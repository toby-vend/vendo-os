## The two camps: overspending vs underspending — [0:00](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=0)

Almost every advertiser is in one of two camps:

- **Overspenders.** Google sits at the very bottom of the funnel. Top-of-funnel demand is generated by Meta, TikTok, affiliates and so on; Google simply captures that bottom-intent demand and claims a very high ROAS. Because the in-platform ROAS looks great, spend gets pushed up even though the extra spend is not genuinely incremental to the business.
- **Underspenders.** These advertisers understand the bottom-of-funnel critique — often too well. They cap Google at, say, $10,000 a month while spending a quarter of a million on Meta, reasoning "Google is just bottom-of-funnel, why scale it?" But they are in categories with large volumes of cold traffic searching relevant terms every day, so they leave genuine scalable demand on the table because they do not trust the platform.

By the end of the video you should know which camp you are in and where spend needs to move.

## The 5 big shifts in Google Ads — [2:15](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=135)

1. **PMax hit its ceiling.** At launch, Google was likely discounting auctions to push adoption, so early results were exceptional. Once everyone weighted spend into PMax, the arbitrage disappeared. Scaling PMax to $100k–$300k/month showed in-platform ROAS holding (e.g. still reporting 5x after doubling budget) while back-end revenue did not move — which triggered incrementality testing and budget redistribution.
2. **Standard shopping is back.** It was phased out of most accounts when PMax arrived, but once PMax hits diminishing returns, continued cold new-customer acquisition requires layering standard shopping back into the structure.
3. **Match type definitions changed.** Exact match now matches to "same meaning" rather than the literal keyword; phrase match got broader; broad match is as broad as ever. This breaks older strategies such as SKAGs (single keyword ad groups), which effectively stopped working around 2020.
4. **Google introduced AI Max and Demand Gen.**
5. **DSAs (Dynamic Search Ads) are being deprecated.**

## Account structure: consolidation beats segmentation — [4:32](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=272)

**First rule of account structure: consolidation will always beat segmentation.** The reasoning:

- Imagine three shopping campaigns with the same products: Campaign A gets 50 conversions per 30 days, Campaign B gets 20, Campaign C gets 10. Each campaign learns largely in isolation (there is a little platform-level data sharing, but treat them as siloed).
- Campaign C is bidding in real time using the psychographic data of only 10 users — so it inherently performs badly. In practice, as conversion volume per campaign falls, ROAS falls with it, and that is usually causal.
- The fix: delete campaigns B and C, move products and budget into A. Now 90 conversions per month flow through one campaign, the model bids more accurately, and ROAS improves.

**So why segment at all?** Because business complexity has to be matched by account complexity. Examples ([6:51](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=411)):

- **Grade C inventory** you need to shift (else discount 80% or give away) would get no spend inside one consolidated campaign — so it earns its own low-priority campaign even though it will not perform well.
- **New arrivals** need to surface at the top of Google Shopping quickly so your range stays seasonally relevant — another justified campaign.
- A **core campaign** carries the rest.

The principle: **stay as consolidated as possible while introducing the commercial realities of the business.** An agency that does not understand your business down to the grade-C-inventory level will either segment for no reason (adding complexity and reducing performance) or over-consolidate and misalign the account with commercial objectives.

**Mental model ([8:47](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=527)): the goal is always one campaign.** That is never realistic, but it is the default — and every additional campaign needs heavy justification.

## The 4 justifications for segmenting — [8:57](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=537)

1. **Brand vs non-brand.** Never let your one PMax campaign absorb brand search. Brand and cold must be separate campaigns — always.
2. **Geography.** Selling in multiple countries should always be segmented at campaign level.
3. **Different margins or efficiency goals.** A 70% gross margin collection cannot share a campaign with a 35% margin collection. On a shared 300% target ROAS, one is barely profitable and the other is highly profitable — and because Google (in most setups) cannot see margin, it optimises to revenue and pushes spend into whichever generates more revenue, even if that is the unprofitable side.
4. **Product categories.** Genuinely different products should not learn together. Example: office chairs and plants — plausibly the same shop, but the audiences are entirely different personas, so the data should be segmented so each learns in isolation.

## Why single keyword ad groups are dead — [10:38](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=638)

- SKAGs do not fit how Google wants accounts structured. Google and Facebook say a lot of things because they want your money, and roughly 80% of it should be ignored — but when they say keyword consolidation is beneficial, *and testing confirms it*, believe it.
- The presenter's agency still inherits SKAG accounts from new clients, migrates them to single topic ad groups, and **always sees better performance**.

**Why SKAGs died ([14:18](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=858)):** ads used to be static — you wrote three headlines and one or two descriptions, chose their order, and that exact ad served on that exact keyword. Total control, so per-keyword ad groups made sense. Now every search ad is a **responsive search ad**: 15 headlines, 4 descriptions, plus other assets, giving roughly **2,700 possible combinations** of one ad. Google split-tests those combinations, and properly testing them needs on the order of **a quarter of a million impressions**. One exact-match keyword in a SKAG will never generate that volume, so the ad sits in learning indefinitely. The only way out of learning is higher volume through each ad group and ad — and because the ad is dynamic, it adjusts itself to each keyword within a themed group anyway.

## Why shopping beats search for eCommerce — [11:42](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=702)

(Note: this video is oriented to eCommerce retail; the channel only works with eCommerce brands. Lead-gen viewers should mentally substitute "search" wherever "shopping" is said.)

- In eCommerce retail, **80–90% of spend should sit in shopping, not search**, because of **pre-click information**.
- A search ad gives a headline, a description nobody reads, and a couple of extensions. Searching "buy red dress size medium for wedding" returns an ad that matches the words but shows no price, no image, no options — you pay $1–$2 per click hoping the landing page matches desire. In visual categories like fashion, that produces many clicks from people who bounce with "this isn't the style I like".
- A shopping ad shows the image, price, title, promos, reviews and brand — and crucially, **five competitors appear alongside you**, so the user compares six listings and clicks the one they prefer. They arrive **pre-bought into your listing being the best**, so conversion rates are higher.
- Therefore shopping will outperform search in almost any eCommerce business **until you fully saturate the shopping network**, at which point you expand into search for additional volume.

## Single topic ad groups explained — [13:19](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=799)

Service-business example (search campaigns matter most for services): three ad groups — **general plumbing**, **emergency plumbing**, **affordable plumbing**.

- Under the old SKAG model, each ad group bid on one keyword in phrase or exact match.
- Under **STAG (single topic ad groups)**, each ad group is a *topic* containing **10–20 themed keywords** relevant to it, with ads tuned to that theme so they are contextually relevant to every keyword in the group.
- This gives responsive ads the impression volume they need to exit learning, while the dynamic ad assembly keeps relevance per keyword.

## Rapid-fire account mistakes — [16:57](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1017)

Also covered just before this: **account hygiene** ([16:08](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=968)) — use a consistent naming convention: agency tag, region, campaign type (PMax/shopping/search), audience (cold/brand/feeder), and optionally the goal (usually omitted since it is almost always sales/conversions).

Common mistakes:

1. **GA4 used as the primary conversion source.** GA4 events miss roughly **10–15% (sometimes more)** of conversions versus direct snippet code on the site.
2. **One asset group on a PMax campaign** (expanded later).
3. **Broad match with Maximise Clicks.** Broad match only works with a smart bidding strategy; on non-smart bidding it does not work.
4. **Display network enabled on search campaigns.** A rookie error — display is arguably one of the worst ad networks, especially for cold traffic.
5. **No negative keywords** — both no branded negatives on cold campaigns, and no negatives generally on search.
6. **Auto-apply recommendations left on.** Google applies changes that are almost never in your interest.

## Performance Max: mechanics and traps — [18:25](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1105)

**PMax is not the enemy** — but default-settings PMax is. Context: the channel previously published a "PMax campaigns are terrible" video, which was true *for its moment*. The test behind that view: take an account where PMax and standard shopping both report 5x ROAS, then scale one by 2x over 30 days. Every time, **the standard shopping ramp produced far better incremental back-end revenue** — even though scaled PMax kept reporting 5x while scaled shopping's reported ROAS fell to 4x or 3x.

**Why:** PMax retargets heavily, so as spend rises it over-credits itself with purchases it did not genuinely drive. Standard shopping drives top-of-funnel traffic that then gets attributed to PMax when PMax retargets and takes the last click.

**PMax done properly requires ([20:31](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1231)):** correct asset group structure, brand exclusions, understanding that audience signals are signals not targeting, and feed-only mode where appropriate.

**Caveats:**

- PMax is great for **smaller accounts** (roughly ≤$15–20k/month) — it gets accounts moving and scaling.
- PMax **hits a ceiling**: beyond a point, incremental back-end returns stop following the platform's numbers.
- The natural responses to the plateau — raise budgets further, or raise the target ROAS — both just push more spend into warm and repeat traffic. Reported ROAS rises, but the campaign becomes "a glorified retargeting campaign".
- Reframe the question from *"should I run PMax?"* to *"what percentage allocation should PMax get vs standard shopping?"* For most mid-to-upper-market eCommerce retail, the answer is **20–40% to PMax**, with most spend through standard shopping ([21:47](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1307)).

## The biggest PMax mistake (one asset group) — [22:11](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1331)

- Setup defaults to a single asset group; lazy setup leaves it that way. An even worse variant: multiple asset groups created but the **listing groups never customised**, so every asset group contains all products anyway and the segmentation is meaningless.
- **Definitions:** an **asset group** is roughly the equivalent of an ad (like different ads in Meta). Shopping placements do not use "ads" — they use the products from the feed, and the product feed inside PMax is called the **listing group**. Each asset group has a connected listing group.
- The asset group's assets (images, headlines, descriptions) power placements on search, display, YouTube side banners, Discover, Gmail and other surfaces. But since most eCommerce performance should come from shopping, the **listing group** — which powers shopping placements — is critical, and must be segmented to match the asset group's theme.

## The 4 reasons to segment asset groups — [24:18](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1458)

(Yes, this appears to contradict "consolidation beats segmentation" — these are the justified exceptions at asset group level.)

1. **Creative relevance.** If a user browsed jeans, retargeting across display/YouTube/etc. should show jeans imagery and headlines, not random products. Same on cold: a segmented jeans asset group is served to people believed to be in-market for jeans, not "anyone interested in the brand".
2. **Audience signals.** These are *not* targeting (a common misconception carried over from old Meta interest targeting). They are **directional signal used early**, before the campaign has conversion data, so Google does not waste budget testing random people. Granular asset groups allow granular signals ("in-market for jeans") rather than a vague "people into fashion".
3. **Search themes.** Same idea as audience signals but keyword-shaped: "if they searched terms like these, consider them".
4. **Visibility into performance breakdowns.** Segmentation lets you see, for example, that tops are outperforming jeans, sanity-check that against seasonality and keyword-planner demand, and redistribute budget. Without asset group segmentation you never get that siloed insight.

**Rules of thumb ([27:06](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1626)):**

- One asset group per **product type** (furniture: chairs, tables, lighting, decor; hardware: hand tools, power tools, fasteners, accessories).
- Always customise each listing group to contain only that asset group's products.
- **At least ~15 products per asset group** (flex by SKU count). **Never one product in an asset group** — it simply will not get spend, even for a hero product.
- Consolidation still beats segmentation: early testing with ~200 asset groups (segmented by listing group, search terms, product type, etc.) showed **simpler structures always performed better**. General rule: **minimum 3, maximum 20 asset groups**; few businesses have the complexity to justify more than 20.

## Search themes and audience signals — [29:03](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1743)

Three things to know:

1. **They are inputs, not targeting.** Adding search themes does not guarantee PMax bids on those terms — it is directional signal only.
2. **Use converting terms, not guesses.** Pull historically converting key terms from your search campaigns' data. Historical account data always beats invention.
3. **They barely matter on mature accounts.** Based on running PMax on 250+ accounts and consulting on ~400 more (7–10 figures): if you are already spending $30k–$100k/month on PMax, tweaking signals and themes "is a one-percenter at best". An agency audit whose headline finding is "your audience signals could be tweaked" will make no material impact.

**Where they do matter: new launches** — a new business, product, category or asset group with no historical data. There, PMax uses the themes and signals as its starting direction. After roughly **30 days of conversion data**, Google effectively bypasses the audience signals; you could remove them without affecting performance.

## Brand exclusions on PMax — [31:10](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1870)

Simply exclude your brand name from PMax. Two mechanisms — **use both** as mutual fail-safes:

1. **Brand list exclusion:** Campaign settings → Brand exclusions → add your brand; Google indexes all related branded terms and applies the list. (For about a year this was the only way.)
2. **Negative keywords on PMax** (recently rolled out): add your brand terms directly as negatives.

If a negative keyword variation is missed, the brand list catches it; if negatives fail, the brand list still protects you — and vice versa.

## Ranking what matters most in PMax — [32:08](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1928)

1. **The GMC feed** — titles, descriptions, imagery (assuming eCommerce and PMax putting most spend into shopping).
2. **Brand exclusions** — without them, PMax campaigns "are just retargeting campaigns".
3. **Asset and listing group structure, plus asset quality** — terrible headlines/images/descriptions make terrible ads.
4. **Bidding strategy.**
5. **Audience signals and search themes.**

## Feed-only PMax explained — [33:05](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=1985)

- **What it is:** delete the asset groups, leaving only the listing group (products from the GMC feed). PMax then can only place on **shopping** and **display retargeting** (product tiles only — it has no headlines or descriptions to build anything else). This is effectively what the old **Smart Shopping** campaign type was.
- **Two settings that destroy feed-only campaigns** ([33:55](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2035)): turn **off URL expansion** (or it scrapes URLs and auto-builds asset groups) and turn **off automatically created assets**.
- **Why run it:** to force spend into the shopping placement when you do not want PMax on search, YouTube, Gmail, etc.
- **War story ([34:45](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2085)):** a client spending a couple of hundred thousand per month on PMax saw clicks spike and ROAS drop ~20%. The campaign had gone rogue — YouTube spend jumped from 2% to ~10%, and ~10% went into cold Gmail placements. Gmail placements are especially misleading: the "ad" looks like an email at the top of the inbox, and Google counts *opening the email* as a click — not an outbound click to the site. So you get thousands of apparent 10-cent clicks that never reach your website, plus angry customers complaining they unsubscribed and are still "getting emails". Pivoting that PMax to feed-only made those placements physically impossible, fixed the issue, and the account kept scaling.
- You can also mix: multiple PMax campaigns per category, some feed-only, some not.

## Target ROAS and the bidding trap — [36:47](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2207)

- Standard progression: launch on **max conversion value** to accumulate data, then roll into **target ROAS**. That is broadly correct.
- **What tROAS actually changes:** max conversion value performance is volatile — big bets that sometimes pay off, sometimes lose. tROAS flattens it. **Average performance over time is roughly the same; the difference is day-to-day reliability**, which most businesses want.
- **The trap ([38:21](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2301)):** you set tROAS at 400% (trailing ROAS was ~420–430%). It stabilises. You cannot or do not want to scale budget, so you squeeze: 425%… 450%… eventually ~500%, where spend starts pulling back to equilibrium. Zoomed out it looks like brilliant media buying — from a 4x ROAS to a 5x, then 6x, at the same $1,000/day. **In reality the earlier position was probably better**, because it was reaching more cold traffic.
- **The mechanism — the bullseye analogy ([39:50](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2390)):** picture the whole population as a circle. The subset you can hit a 7x on is small and full of warm people — site visitors, existing customers, people who saw your Meta ads. To scale spend, the circle must widen into colder users at worse efficiency. Raising tROAS does the opposite: **the circle shrinks**. Efficiency looks better, but you are not genuinely acquiring new customers — you are tightening the pool.

## Feeder strategies — [40:48](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2448)

- **Premise:** run one campaign with a very low target ROAS — as low as **50% (0.5x)** — that targets the *entire* pool of relevant searchers, and a second campaign with a high tROAS (e.g. **600%**) that only wins warm, in-market, likely-to-buy users. The low-tROAS campaign drives and captures traffic; the high-tROAS campaign converts it on the second or third click. Top-of-funnel feeds bottom-of-funnel.
- **The arbitrage ([41:40](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2500)):** in any keyword pool (say, everyone searching for dresses), everyone with a high-tROAS strategy is bidding on the same visibly-high-intent half — people with recent purchase-intent searches and site visits. The other half look low-intent to Google (first-time searchers, no site history), so **almost nobody is entering those auctions**. You can bid on them extremely cheaply. Much of that traffic will be poor, but plenty is good, and it funnels into the bottom-of-funnel campaign to convert. This works well on many accounts.
- **Standard configuration ([43:59](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2639)):** standard shopping as the cold/feeder campaign (it is simply better at reaching cold audiences), PMax as the high-tROAS retargeting/converter campaign.

## Why PMax overattributes — [43:00](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2580)

- PMax was sold in 2022 as the replacement for Smart Shopping. Three years on, measured results — and what the owners of some of the biggest Google Ads agencies say behind closed doors — agree: **PMax substantially overattributes. 50% or more of PMax conversions are repeat or warm customers PMax did not genuinely acquire.**
- Correspondingly, **standard shopping substantially underattributes** when it sits next to PMax, because PMax takes the credit. Net effect: PMax looks like it is scaling well while standard shopping quietly does the cold acquisition work holding it up.

## Standard shopping strategies — [44:03](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2643)

All previous rules still apply (consolidation over segmentation; segment only on the four justifications). Three in-market strategies that work:

1. **Top sellers vs everything else.** One campaign holds your top sellers, another holds the rest — forcing Pareto's principle (~80% of spend into the top 20% of products) while keeping testing budget on the long tail so new winners can roll up. Only appropriate for **large SKU counts outside fashion**; wrong for fashion and wrong for 5–10-product stores.
2. **The feeder strategy** (as above): standard shopping feeding a high-tROAS PMax.
3. **The labeliser (à la "flow roast" tiering).** Split ad groups/campaigns by product performance tier: **over-index, index, near-index, under-index, no-index** (above expectation, on target, just below, below, zero). Small catalogues can do this manually with custom labels; otherwise use an **automated labeliser script** that re-tiers products on 30-day rolling performance in real time, with a bidding strategy and budget per tier. **Not recommended for most businesses** — it adds heavy segmentation for limited upside — but with **thousands of SKUs** it becomes an effective management system.

## Search campaigns (and when they matter) — [46:15](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2775)

- For ~90% of eCommerce brands, ~90% of focus belongs on shopping. The exception: **eCommerce with a B2B component**. Business buyers intuitively know B2B does not happen on the shopping network — a café owner sourcing bulk tea will not click a $5 consumer shopping listing because it does not match the bulk/wholesale price in their head. They click search ads instead. Example: selling bulk eyelashes to technicians — search outperforms shopping.
- **Components of a modern search campaign ([47:47](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2867)):**
  - **Topic ad groups**, not keyword ad groups.
  - **Negative keywords:** the first 30 days are the highest-leverage window — the campaign goes out broad and places on plenty of irrelevant terms. Review negatives **weekly** in month one (daily on very high budgets), then drop to weekly–fortnightly. Do not over-tweak: every change resets learnings slightly.
  - **A universal negative keyword list** applied at account level so every new campaign inherits it — job-related queries (jobs, careers, interns, hiring, salary) and similar irrelevant terms. Lists are easy to find online or have an LLM draft one.
  - **Responsive search ads maths ([49:00](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2940)):** 2,700 combinations × even a generously low 100 impressions per combination = **270,000 impressions to exit learning**. Implication: minimise segmentation, or your RSAs stay in learning indefinitely — as they do in most accounts.

## Landing pages: the biggest hidden lever — [49:43](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=2983)

The largest lever in search campaigns is not in the account at all — it is the landing page.

- **The economics:** you pay a CPC, drive traffic to the site, and revenue = conversion rate × average order value. That yields ROAS.
- **CPC is largely a vanity metric ([50:26](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3026)).** Across large datasets, CPC and ROAS show very little relationship — high-CPC/high-ROAS accounts and the reverse both exist, at product and campaign level. Only the extremes are meaningful ($100 CPCs will never be profitable; 10-cent clicks are junk traffic). In the middle, CPC tells you nothing, because **click quality varies** — and quality is determined by the automated bidding you are trusting.
- **Google is ultimately an auction ([51:50](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3110)):** whoever can pay most to win the auction gets the listing, the click and the conversion. How do you afford to pay the most? **By having the highest conversion rate and AOV.** If you convert at double your competitors' rate, you can pay double per click for the same economics. Everything in-platform — segmentation, asset groups, signals, feed optimisation — is really about entering better auctions and improving quality score so your effective bid is artificially advantaged. But a competitor with better conversion rate and AOV can still beat you **with worse ads and worse structure**, simply because they can afford to.
- **The practical tactic almost nobody does ([53:35](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3215)): split test landing pages.** In an ad group, duplicate the ad: ad 1 → landing page 1, ad 2 → landing page 2. Monitor conversion rate over 30–60 days, keep the winner, rotate in a new challenger (ad 3 → landing page 3), and repeat. Average conversion rate climbs, letting you bid more; add AOV optimisation and revenue per visitor rises — which is what unlocks scale.
- Two side notes ([54:23](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3263)): exclude brand on search campaigns too, and for bidding generally use a smart strategy (target ROAS, max conversions or similar).

## Bidding strategies at the account level — [54:33](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3273)

- **Manual CPC / Maximise Clicks:** for new accounts with no conversion data you cannot use smart bidding, so these are forced starting points. Established businesses should not be on them — barring rare, deliberate agency plays to exploit an arbitrage. Get off them as fast as possible.
- Then graduate into **Maximise Conversions** or **Maximise Conversion Value**, and later their targeted versions: **tCPA is a subcomponent of max conversions; tROAS is a subcomponent of max conversion value**.

## Expansive vs restrictive bidding — [56:31](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3391)

- **Expansive** (max conversions / max conversion value): free to enter any auction. Benefit — you win auctions you otherwise would not; drawback — you lose auctions you otherwise would not. Higher-risk model.
- **Restrictive** (tCPA / tROAS): only enters auctions where the efficiency target looks achievable.
- **Useful conceptualisation — testing budget ([56:25](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3385)):** think of max conversion value as allocating ~30% of budget to *testing* — new keywords, audiences, placements, regions, psychographic data points that are adjacent to what has converted rather than proven. Those tests discover new working audiences, which is what unlocks scale. Restrictive strategies run at ~0% testing budget — though this really depends on how aggressive the target is: tROAS 4.5 while achieving 4x means 0% testing; tROAS 2 while achieving 5x behaves almost like max conversion value.
- Restrictive is more efficient but silos you to one audience that never evolves with the business or platform — **staying on tROAS permanently is dangerous**. The recommendation: **rotate between expansive and restrictive over extended periods** to avoid data silos and negative feedback loops without permanently sacrificing efficiency.

## Max conversions vs max conversion value — [59:48](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3588)

In eCommerce, choose **value ~90% of the time**. Max conversions optimises for the lowest CPA, which systematically pushes your **cheapest product**: a $40 t-shirt might achieve a $20 CPA while a $200 jumper costs $60 per order. Even when the jumper's ROAS is *better* (3.5x vs 2x in the example), the campaign does not care — it is optimising order count, not value — so budget floods into the t-shirt. Optimising for value avoids spending into products you do not want to push.

## The target ROAS death spiral — [1:00:10](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3610)

An extremely common pattern: account spend slowly trails off and campaigns die. Mechanism:

1. ROAS dips (seasonality, a good product goes out of stock, anything) while tROAS stays fixed. The campaign stops hitting target, so **spend pulls back** — the bullseye circle shrinks.
2. tROAS models primarily on the **last 30–90 days of conversion data**. As conversions fall (say 100 → 80), the model has a smaller sample, **small-sample bias** worsens targeting, conversions fall further, target is missed harder, spend pulls back more, volume drops again — and bidding accuracy degrades in a loop.
3. Left alone, the whole campaign declines and dies. **If you pull tROAS back, pull it back aggressively** — timid reductions do not break the spiral and the account keeps declining.

The presenter sees onboarded accounts mid-spiral constantly; knowing the mechanics is the difference between rescuing the account and watching it flatline.

## Portfolio bidding strategies — [1:02:47](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3767)

- A **portfolio bidding strategy** is set at the account level and applied across multiple campaigns. (A similar capability exists in SA360, but that is enterprise-only.)
- **Killer feature: merged learnings.** Remember campaigns A/B/C learning in isolation? A portfolio strategy applied to all of them **takes the silos down** — they learn together. This is the fix if you need segmentation for commercial reasons but want pooled data.
- **Second benefit: max/min CPC caps** on top of tROAS or max value. Example: high-end furniture, where auctions can hit $10–$30 per click — you can set the target ROAS *and* a max CPC so the campaign never pays $30 for a user regardless of intent.
- **Smart bidding exploration** ([1:04:32](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3872)) — search-only setting (not on PMax/shopping): even under tROAS, the campaign may go 10–30% outside the target in bands to test. Effectively a middle ground between restrictive and expansive — you hit target while still making learning plays. Recommended if you run search.
- **"Limited by budget"** ([1:05:24](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3924)): an *indicator*, not a rule, that more auctions were available. It appears on virtually every account at every spend level — even $5–10k/day PMax in a small Australian market. Do not treat it as the Bible for incremental spend.
- **Budget changes** ([1:05:53](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=3953)): the 20%-increment rule is real on Google. Meta and TikTok tolerate faster moves; Google is a more stable, consistent platform that wants slow, methodical changes. Doubling budgets overnight or halving a tCPA overnight is a bad idea. Bringing budgets *down* quickly is generally fine with little instability; going up (and changing bidding strategies) should be slow.

## GMC feed optimisation — [1:06:43](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=4003)

Arguably one of the most important levers in shopping: **the feed is how shopping knows which key terms to place on, and it feeds your quality score**, which sets where your bids land.

### Product title structure — [1:07:07](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=4027)

Most GMC titles are unoptimised — this is heavily under-leveraged. No golden rule, but:

- **Default (bad):** `Brand | Nathan Dress | Size 12 | Colour`. The brand is meaningless if brand terms are excluded (and it already appears at the bottom of the listing); the product name "Nathan" means nothing to anyone not already product-aware; "dress" is the first relevant word but says nothing about *what kind* of dress; size is a nice-to-have at best.
- **Fix:** push the brand to the back (or drop it entirely if nobody knows you; keep it if you are a big retailer), and put **relevant keywords at the front** — for the algorithm first, the user second. E.g. prepend "Formal Dress". You can lead with keywords, or lead with product name/type then keywords.
- **Keywords come from data, not imagination ([1:09:16](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=4156)):** build titles from **search term reports** — historically converting queries in the account. If "formal dress" converts brilliantly and this is a formal dress, it goes at the front.
- **Pack in attributes** (within title length limits) — they broaden the queries you rank for, e.g. including "blue" means "blue dress" searches now prioritise you.
- **CPG example:** "creatine powder" becomes `Dissolvable Creatine Powder | Clear | Flavoured | Portable | Brand` — each term chosen from keyword research showing it performs.
- **Variant hack ([1:10:32](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=4232)):** if a product has variants (50g / 100g / 500g), each variant can get its own shopping listing with **different titles, photos and descriptions** — despite pointing at the same landing page. Focus each variant on a different keyword pool to maximise your surface area across Google.

### Image optimisation in the feed — [1:11:16](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=4276)

Two fixes:

1. **Aspect ratio.** Common fashion problem: 9:16 images get auto-cropped badly in the feed. Override with a **supplementary feed of 4:5 images** — now Google's preferred default ratio.
2. **The image is the click-driver.** It occupies ~70% of the listing's real estate; besides price and title, it decides the click. But do not blindly maximise CTR — the image works like a **hook on Facebook**, or Eugene Schwartz's view of headlines: the job is not to hook everyone, but to hook *the right person*. In practice, within brand constraints, that mostly means **building contrast against the competing listings** shown alongside you — if competitors all use white-background shots, use a different background colour so you stand out on the shelf.

### Other feed items — [1:13:08](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=4388)

- Descriptions and all remaining attributes should already be clean — it is a one-time setup; if it is not done, do it.
- **One feed per currency.** A common audit finding: every region running through one feed in one currency. Google then shows a live currency conversion on the listing ("converted from AUD/USD plus tax"), which signals an international store and **kills both CTR and conversion rate** — often when the store is not even really "international", just misconfigured.

## The 90-day rollout plan — [1:13:54](https://youtu.be/DQKF8O1ZcPA?t=4434)

1. **Tracking.** No GA4 events as primary conversions; direct/back-end events with enhanced conversions on; auto-tagging on; everything up to scratch.
2. **Structure map.** How many campaigns do we actually need? The goal is one; every extra needs a commercial reason. Always split brand vs non-brand. Enforce naming consistency.
3. **PMax check.** Asset groups split by product type; listing groups segmented accordingly; brand exclusions on; audience signals present (not urgent); tROAS not absurdly high (which would make it a pure retargeting campaign); correct bidding strategy per the expansive/restrictive and conversions/value logic.
4. **Wasted-spend audit.** Kill or roll up zombie campaigns ($400/month campaigns that serve no purpose); negative-keyword unprofitable search terms; turn off display on search campaigns.
5. **(Implied step 5 folded into the audit above.)**
6. **Ad asset health check.** Sitelinks, callouts, promos that are not stale — maximise assets on every campaign to increase your ads' on-page real estate; an easy quick win.
7. **Feed check — with a slow rollout.** Optimise titles and images, but **do not swap all titles overnight** — that resets learning phases on every campaign. Roll titles gradually, watch CTR, CPCs and returns in real time, and iterate with different keywords if changes are not making a material impact.

---

## Key takeaways

1. **Consolidation beats segmentation.** The default is one campaign; every additional campaign needs a commercial justification — the only valid four being brand vs non-brand, geography, margin/efficiency differences, and genuinely different product categories.
2. **PMax overattributes — 50%+ of its conversions are warm or repeat customers.** Treat the question as "what % allocation to PMax vs standard shopping" (typically 20–40% PMax), not "should I run PMax".
3. **Standard shopping is back** as the cold-acquisition workhorse: it drives the top-of-funnel volume that PMax then claims credit for.
4. **SKAGs are dead; use single topic ad groups** with 10–20 themed keywords — responsive ads need ~270,000 impressions to exit learning, which one keyword can never supply.
5. **Segment PMax asset groups by product type (min 3, max 20, ~15+ products each)** and always customise the listing groups; never run one default asset group.
6. **Exclude brand everywhere** (brand lists *and* negative keywords on PMax; negatives on search) — unexcluded, PMax is just a retargeting campaign.
7. **Raising target ROAS shrinks your audience to warm traffic** — it flatters reported efficiency while strangling new-customer acquisition, and mis-set targets trigger a death spiral of falling data, worse modelling and collapsing spend.
8. **Optimise for conversion value, not conversions**, in eCommerce — max conversions floods budget into your cheapest products.
9. **Conversion rate and AOV are the real competitive weapons**: Google is an auction, and whoever converts best can outbid everyone else — so split test landing pages relentlessly; CPC in the mid-range is a vanity metric.
10. **The GMC feed is the top PMax/shopping lever**: keyword-led titles built from search term report data, 4:5 imagery with contrast against competitors, per-currency feeds — rolled out slowly to avoid resetting learning.
