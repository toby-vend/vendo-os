# Meta Frequency & Creative Fatigue — Vendo Playbook

How Vendo uses frequency as the leading indicator of creative fatigue and creative-diversity problems in Meta accounts. Frequency warns you **before** performance visibly degrades — it is not a vanity metric. Source: Blue Sense Digital, "The Most Important Metric for Creative Fatigue" (2026).

**Core principle: frequency is the symptom; creative is the cause.** High frequency cannot be fixed at the ad-set or targeting level — only with creative diversity and volume.

## How to apply this at Vendo

- **Any frequency number quoted internally must carry two qualifiers: the date range and the account-structure level.** "Frequency is 3" is meaningless on its own. Vendo standard: **14-day window**, stated level (ad / ad set / campaign).
- **Ecommerce clients (Veltuff, Sword Stall).** Full framework applies. Fast purchase cycles (low AOV) mean healthy frequency sits low; do not read a competitor's or another client's number as a benchmark.
- **Dental and lead-gen clients (Bond Dental, Bright Orthodontics, Zen House).** Treatment decisions (e.g. Invisalign, implants) have long consideration windows — a higher frequency is required and expected, not a warning sign. Benchmark each account against its own strong-performance baseline.
- **When frequency flags red, the fix goes on the creative roadmap, not the media plan**: new angles, personas and offers, plus more creative volume. Targeting tweaks and stacking more of the same ads will not move it.

## The mechanics: what frequency measures

**Frequency = impressions ÷ reach** — how many times the average user saw the ads over the selected period.

Two things change the number structurally:

1. **Date range.** The same account reads differently over 7, 14 and 30 days. Cold users are typically served over a few days (up to ~14 for longer-consideration products), then serving stops; retargeting keeps serving the same people indefinitely, so its frequency climbs the longer the window. The 14-day standard captures Meta's natural cold-serving cycle while exposing retargeting behaviour.
2. **Account-structure level.** Frequency always increases as you move up the structure (ad < ad set < campaign), because each level encapsulates more of the audience's total exposure. Two ads each at 1 can roll up to an ad-set frequency of ~1.4 and a campaign frequency of ~2. The difference is structural, not noise.

## The four rules

### Rule 1 — Ad-level frequency should never exceed 2 (14 days, cold campaigns)

Above 2, one of two things is wrong: the creative is fatiguing (over-served to the same people), or the ad has quietly drifted into bottom-of-funnel retargeting instead of reaching new users. Around 90% of the time the check passes — it is a fast scan that occasionally surfaces a genuine red flag worth troubleshooting.

### Rule 2 — Read new-audience frequency only

Blended frequency lies. Existing customers and engaged audiences are served far more often, so their high frequency inflates the overall figure. Segment down to new-audience frequency before making any judgement.

### Rule 3 — Frequency is relative to average time to purchase; never compare brand to brand

A 4-day purchase cycle needs only one to three exposures — low frequency is natural. A 14-day cycle needs more exposures to stay top of mind — higher frequency is required. A high-ticket furniture brand can be healthy at a 14-day frequency of 5; a £70-AOV fashion brand may need to stay under ~2.7. Above the ad level there is no universal benchmark: establish each account's own baseline during strong-performance periods and measure drift against that.

### Rule 4 — Fix frequency at the creative level, not the ad-set level

Meta takes a creative and finds the audience it believes will resonate. If every ad carries the same message, angle and offer to the same person, every ad finds the same pool — stacking more of the same ads leaves frequency climbing. The durable fix is ads that talk to **different people**: new angles, personas and offers that open genuinely new audience pools, plus more creative volume (low volume is itself a frequency driver).

## The hidden distribution problem

Frequency is a **mean**, and the real distribution is heavily skewed — not the bell curve people assume. Meta removed the frequency-breakdown report years ago, so the distribution is invisible, but a common real-world shape is: average 2.4 while ~10% of users are hit 11+ times. That heavy tail — typically existing customers pulled into targeting, plus users Meta scores as very high-intent — drags the mean up and represents wasted spend you cannot directly see. Treat the average with suspicion; a "fine" number can hide substantial over-serving.

## Short-term relief valves (use knowingly)

**Whitelisting ads** (running through an owned page that reads as third-party) and **partnership ads** (dual-handle with an influencer, unlocking their audience) both mechanically reduce frequency by reaching new pools through new handles. Both are a plaster: they buy time but do not fix the underlying creative-diversity problem.

## Quick reference

| Check | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| Date window | 14 days, always stated |
| Structure level | Always stated (ad / ad set / campaign; number rises as you go up) |
| Ad-level ceiling (cold) | 2.0 — above this, fatigue or hidden retargeting |
| Ad set / campaign benchmark | Account-relative — baseline from own strong periods |
| Audience cut | New audiences only; blended numbers are inflated |
| Cross-brand comparison | Never — frequency tracks time to purchase |
| The fix | Creative diversity + volume, not targeting changes |
