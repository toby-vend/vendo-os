**CHAPTER 1**

# **Breaking Free: The Squat Practice Revolution**

Looking for advice on how to start a squat? Then I’m your man – been there, done that and got the T-shirt to prove it! Not literally, of course. But if I did have such a T-shirt, it would probably say something like ‘I escaped Corporate dentistry and all I got was this thriving dental practice and my conscience back!’. Anyway, welcome to my story. Which might very soon be your story, but without, I hope, some of the rollercoaster ride I’m about to share with you.  
First, before we start to get to know each other, you should probably know that I never set out to become a revolutionary in the dental world. Far from it.  
In fact, my graduation yearbook at school may very well have proclaimed that I was ‘the person least likely to succeed’.  
Yep, I was that kid. Getting in a little trouble here and there – maybe even nearly getting kicked out once. (Okay, twice then!) Not stupid, just a round peg in a square hole. A bad fit for a one-size-fits-all educational ‘system’. A sign of things to come, perhaps. But somewhere along the line, I straightened myself out, at least just enough to get those all-important straight A’s in science.  
‘Important’, because now Dad and I could have THAT conversation. The conversation that every hard-working immigrant father has with his kids. “So Bobby, what’s it going to be – doctor, dentist, accountant, or lawyer?” he quizzed, sipping on his tea and reaching for another cake rusk.  
I took a sip of my tea too, more to buy a few seconds of thinking time than to quench my thirst. For a split second, the naughty kid from school nearly blurted out ‘train driver’ just for a laugh, but comedy and career conversation don’t mix well in an Asian home so I quickly weighed up my experience to date – work experience with a few local dentists had been fun at least way more fun than I’d seen our neighbourhood doctor having, on call leaving home at all times of the day and night clutching his scuffed and battered Gladstone bag. Ten seconds later, I’d concluded that dentistry seemed to be a better lifestyle. Meaning the dentists I’d come across worked fewer hours, drove nicer cars and laughed more than the doctor. So I found myself saying ‘dentist’.  
Now, before you rush to conclude that this is my happy ever after story and consider flipping through a few pages to get to the instruction part of the book, I’d ask you to lean in and listen closely to what happened next – because the rollercoaster ride is about to start!  
Getting into dental school was all uphill. I swaggered into the interviews and quickly learnt the meaning of the old adage ‘if you fail to prepare, you’re preparing to fail’. Turns out loads of people show up with good grades. But they match it with a ‘reason why I want to be a dentist story’ that would make Simon Sinek beam.  
Me? I was just trying to keep the family happy.  
So I was rejected. Once. Then twice. Eventually, four humiliating times.  
But Dad didn’t flinch. “Try harder, Bobby,” he said, sipping another cup of tea whilst lustfully eyeing the plate of cake rusk.  
Trying harder meant changing my story. Selling myself. Another career-building lesson (- have you started making notes?). So I locked myself in my bedroom and for a whole box of cake rusk, I wrote and then rewrote the most desperate, heartfelt letter of my life to the Admissions Tutor at Birmingham University. The kind of letter where you pour your soul onto paper and hope someone on the other end actually cares. Miraculously, someone did and one morning a week later, an interview offer dropped through my letterbox. This time I prepared like my life depended on it ... and this time got in!  
Now, I’d like to be able to say that this was the start of the ‘and Bobby lived happily ever after’ part of the story, but we are still some ways off – you see I hated every minute of dental school. And there are a lot of minutes in 5 years. And close shaves, where I seemed to be just one more bad attempt at drilling a cavity away from being kicked out.  
Ironically though, my best friend and I, the two students who fought consistently for last place in class, are now among the most successful practitioners from our year – we’ll talk more about this lesson later.  
Graduation was a turning point – I got my first training place and discovered I actually enjoyed dentistry. Turns out it wasn’t the profession I hated; it was once again, how I’d been taught.  
After bouncing around a bit, I landed at a practice that felt like home. The principal was exceptional – the mentor I’d never had in dental school. For seven years, I thrived in what felt like a dental family. We worked hard, supported each other, and I actually looked forward to Mondays.  
Then came the announcement.  
“I’ve decided to sell the practice,” the principal told us one Friday afternoon. The room went silent. “Don’t worry,” he assured us with a beaming smile. “Everything will stay the same. Your jobs are secure.”  
A collective sigh could be heard in the room. At first, I thought it was a sigh of relief but as I looked at the faces of my colleagues ... my friends ... my dental family ... what I saw was a look of dismay.  
At the time, I didn’t understand why. But within months the reason for that initial dismay became obvious, as one day at a time, the soul was being sucked out of the place. The new corporate overlords brought in their spreadsheets, their metrics, and their ‘every man for himself’ culture.  
I still remember the morning I walked in and found three patients missing from my schedule. When I asked the receptionist what happened, she looked uncomfortable.  
“Dr. X moved them to his diary,” she whispered.  
These weren’t just any appointments – they were implant consultations I’d built rapport with. When I confronted my old ‘friend’, he just shrugged: “I need to hit my targets this month”.  
In the old practice, this would have been unthinkable. Now it was just Tuesday.  
Soon, everything changed. We sat through meetings where corporate managers discussed patients as ‘units of revenue’. Treatment plans started looking suspiciously uniform regardless of patient needs. The warm greeting at reception became a rigid sales funnel. Team lunches disappeared. Bowling nights stopped. One by one, our team left in search of a new ‘home’.  
One particularly grim Friday afternoon, I sat in my car in the parking lot, staring at the corporate logo that had replaced our old practice sign. A text from the manager excitedly reminded us about the new ‘performance metrics’ being implemented on Monday.  
I turned off my phone, drove home, grabbed a packet of cake rusk and a cup of tea, went up to my bedroom and drafted my resignation letter.  
I was done. Corporate had robbed me of the best time of my life. In fact, I was so dejected I actually walked away from dentistry completely and started dabbling in property development with my family. But something was missing. I’d fallen in love with dentistry, at least the way my old principal was doing dentistry. He’d developed a team of caring, ethical human beings serving our patients and enjoying ourselves at the same time. NOT money-making robots.  
Do you sense a tea and rusk story coming on?  
“Bobby, what should we do with that property in Leamington – got any ideas?” asked my dad as we drank our Sunday morning tea.  
I’d driven past it a hundred times without really seeing it. But that morning, something clicked. I put down my cup and really looked at my dad.  
“Well,” I said slowly, feeling a bit like that cheeky kid back at school all over again, “What if we turned it into a dental practice?”  
Dad raised an eyebrow. “Your practice?”  
The words hung in the air between us. My practice. The possibility felt both terrifying and strangely inevitable. “I’ll get a new box of cake rusk,” he said, smiling. A smile I’d seen just once before – the day I graduated from dental school.  
As I lay in bed that night, doubt started creeping in. Who was I to think I could run a successful practice? I’d barely survived dental school. I’d walked away from dentistry completely not long ago. And you could make horror movies based on the stories I’d heard about squat practices failing.  
I needed to talk to someone who knew what they were doing. Someone who’d done it before.  
Who better than my former mentor, I mused. A couple of years after escaping the corporate nightmare he’d inadvertently created by selling his practice, he’d gone on to open his own squat. Maybe he could tell me if I was completely mad for considering this.  
We met at his new practice – a beautiful space that hummed with happy patients and staff. It had the same feeling as his old practice. He’d done it again, so it was no accident. He either had the magic touch or a plan. I hoped it was a plan, because if he did, and he would share it with me, guide and advise me, then surely I could do the same, I thought as I waited for him in reception.  
The meeting was short and sweet and can be summarised in his response when I tentatively told him my idea. “Bobby, why haven’t you done this sooner?” he said, without hesitation.  
I blinked. “You think I could do it?”  
“I know you can,” he said, leaning back in his chair. “You’re clinically solid. You connect with patients. You’ve seen what works and what doesn’t. And that location you described? Perfect for what you want to build.”  
That conversation shifted something in me. If my mentor – who knew my strengths and weaknesses better than almost anyone – if he believed I could do this, then maybe I actually could.  
I walked out of his office and called the bank that same afternoon.  
Now let me be clear about what followed: if my squat practice journey were a movie, it would be firmly in the disaster genre.  
Picture this: Six months later, I’m standing in pouring rain, staring at what was supposed to be the back extension of my future practice. There are half-built walls with no roof. Construction materials scattered everywhere. The project manager is nowhere to be found. And I’ve just discovered my builders have been charging me VAT without being VAT registered – committing fraud right under my nose.  
I now had a half-built practice, no builders, and a rapidly approaching deadline with the bank. Oh, and did I mention I was still working three to four days a week as an associate because I needed the income to fund this increasingly nightmarish project?  
The hits kept coming. A neighbour who’d taken a dislike to our construction reported us to Health and Safety. An inspection swiftly shut down the entire site.  
“You can’t continue building without proper structural engineering drawings,” the building control officer told me flatly.  
“No problem,” I said confidently. “I’ll get them from my structural engineer.”  
Except my structural engineer turned out to be mates with the fraudulent builders I’d just fired.  
“The builders are my client, not you,” he informed me coldly. “I’m not releasing those drawings.”  
Building control wouldn’t let us continue without them. Without continued construction, the bank might pull my funding. Without funding, three years of savings and my family’s investment would evaporate.  
And if that weren’t enough, my dad – who I was relying on to help manage the project given his experience in property development – well, he’d developed health issues and was hospitalised three times during the construction period.  
I remember the night before my CQC inspection – the make-or-break moment for any new practice. My phone rang at 11 PM. It was my mother, her voice trembling: “Bobby, the doctors say Dad might not make it through the night.”  
I stood in my half-finished practice, mentally calculating how many regulations we still needed to address before the inspector arrived at 9 AM, while simultaneously processing that my father might be dying.  
In moments like these, your mind searches for escape hatches. Twice during this period, I seriously considered walking away from the whole project. Just cutting my losses. Going back to being an associate. Steady paycheque. No builders. No inspections. No sleepless nights.  
“I don’t think I can do this anymore,” I told my wife one particularly dark evening, my head in my hands. “Maybe I should just go back to being an associate.”  
She sat next to me, quiet for a moment. Then she took my hand.  
“You’re not giving up,” she said simply. “This isn’t who you are.”  
So we pushed through. Somehow, we found new builders who pieced together what the previous ones had left behind. We found a way to get our hands on those structural drawings. My father stabilised. We passed the CQC inspection by the skin of our teeth.  
And finally, finally, after delays and disasters that had added months to our timeline and thousands to our budget, we were ready to open our doors.  
I still remember every minute of Avenue Dental’s first day. We had a list of patients to call, and my palms were sweating as I picked up the phone to make the first call. What if no one booked? What if we’d gone through all this for nothing?  
By lunchtime, we’d collected over £500 in appointment fees.  
That first trickle of patients soon became a steady stream. Not without challenges, mind you. My first hire – a dental nurse with years of experience – turned out to be a nightmare. Eighteen months in, she demanded, “I want to work four days but get paid for five, plus full-time holiday allowance.” When I refused, she created so much drama that I had to let her go.  
Building the right team took longer than I expected – nearly two years to get the perfect mix of people who shared my vision. But looking back now, every struggle was worth it.  
Today, I run a practice that truly reflects my values. I have complete clinical freedom. My team amazes me daily – they really are worth their weight in gold. I practice dentistry the way I believe it should be practised.  
And most importantly? I actually look forward to Mondays again.  
Today, I only have one regret - not taking the leap and doing my squat sooner.  
Which brings me to why I wrote this book.

## **Why I Wrote This Book**

So there I was, finally, a practice owner. You might think this is where I tell you how brilliant I am, how I’ve got it all figured out, and now I’m going to graciously bestow my wisdom upon you.  
Not quite.  
The truth is, I stumbled through this process like a blindfolded man in an obstacle course. I made mistakes – expensive, stressful mistakes – that I now know were completely avoidable.  
That’s why I wrote this book. Because in those darkest moments – standing in the rain looking at my roofless practice, facing down fraudulent builders, wondering how I’d make payroll – I would have given anything for a guide written by someone who’d been there, scraped their knees, and lived to tell the tale.  
During my journey, I’ve met countless dentists just like you. Talented, dedicated professionals who dream of breaking free from corporate dentistry but find themselves stuck in a loop of “I’d love to, but ...”  
“But I don’t have enough business knowledge ...”  
“But I wouldn’t know where to locate my practice ...”  
“But I can’t imagine how I’d find good staff ...”  
“But what if I can’t attract enough patients ...”  
“But what if I fail and lose everything?”  
Sound familiar? I had all those same doubts, plus a few bonus ones I created just for myself. (I’m an overachiever like that.)  
Here’s what I’ve realised: most of what holds us back isn’t the practical obstacles – it’s the fear of the unknown.  
When you don’t know what you don’t know, your mind fills in the blanks with worst-case scenarios. My brain was exceptionally creative in this department.  
This book exists to make the unknown known. To turn those shadowy, terrifying ‘what ifs’ into clear, manageable steps.  
So, this isn’t one of those practice management books written by someone who opened a practice thirty years ago when all you needed was a drill, a chair, and a sign on the door. Nor is it written by a consultant who’s spent more time in boardrooms than dental surgeries.  
This is the resource I wish I’d had when I was standing in the rain with my half-built practice, wondering if I’d made the biggest mistake of my life. It’s the guide I would have given my younger self, complete with tea and cake rusk stains on the important pages.  
Most importantly, this book is about helping you avoid my biggest regret – not doing this sooner.  
Because here’s the thing: despite all the drama, all the setbacks, all the moments of doubt, opening my own practice was the best professional decision I’ve ever made. The freedom to practice dentistry the way I believe it should be practised – priceless. Building a team that feels like family (the good kind, not the ones who steal your patients) – transformative. Creating a space where patients actually enjoy coming to the dentist – deeply satisfying.  
When my wife told me, “This isn’t who you are” during my moment of doubt, she was right. And if you’ve read this far, chances are, being stuck in corporate dentistry isn’t who you are either.  
So let’s get on with it, shall we?

**CHAPTER 2**

# **The Three Pillars of Squat Practice Success**

Here's what I didn't know when I started.  
I didn't know why some squat practices thrived and others struggled. I didn't know whether what happened to me – the fraudulent builders, the first-hire disaster, the nail-biting first phone call – was normal. I didn't know if my practice worked because of what I'd done or in spite of it.  
For the first two years, I assumed I'd got lucky.  
Then, as other dentists started reaching out – "Bobby, how did you do it?" "What would you do differently?" "Would you help me?" – I started paying attention. Not just to my own practice, but to theirs. Dozens of them. Some soared. Some limped along. A few closed within a year or two.  
At first I thought the differences were clinical skill, or capital, or location, or luck. They weren't. I've seen clinically brilliant dentists with £200,000 in the bank open in beautiful locations and close within eighteen months. I've seen average clinicians with tight budgets open in unglamorous places and build thriving practices doing half a million a year.  
The difference wasn't any one variable. It was three. And they kept coming up. Every single time.  
Every practice that thrived had them. Every practice that struggled was missing at least one.  
I call them the Three Pillars.

## **The Certainty Compass**

Picture me, clipboard in hand, trying to choose builders for my practice. Did I check references? Not really. Did I verify their VAT registration? Er, no. Did I have a proper contract? Does an email saying "we start on Monday" count?  
Here's what I did have: a rapidly ticking clock, mounting pressure from the bank, and a desperate need to just get things moving.  
My decision-making process resembled a blindfolded person throwing darts – wildly hoping that something would hit the target. The results were predictably disastrous.  
What I've since realised is that most of the expensive mistakes in squat practice ownership – mine and everyone else's – aren't bad luck. They're the predictable consequence of making big decisions without a framework. A framework that accounts for the way our own minds trip us up when the stakes are high and the emotions are running hot.  
Because here's what I've learned from watching dentists make these decisions: intelligence doesn't save you. Experience doesn't save you. Clinical excellence doesn't save you. What saves you is having a system that catches you before you fall into one of three very common traps.  
The Prestige Virus – that little voice that says, "I need the fanciest equipment in the poshest location to impress my colleagues." (Spoiler alert: patients care more about your care than your coffee machine.) I've watched talented dentists sink tens of thousands into locations chosen entirely for how they'd sound at a dinner party.  
The Quick-Fix Fever – the desperate rush to "just get started" that leads to cutting corners. Like hiring the first builders who showed up with a quote. I've seen dentists lose six figures because they were too impatient to do a week's worth of due diligence.  
Analysis Paralysis Syndrome – the opposite problem. Gathering so much information that you never actually make a decision. I see this in dentists who've been "researching locations" for three years. Meanwhile, the opportunities have come and gone.  
The Certainty Compass is the framework I developed to navigate the three decisions that matter most – which business model, where to locate, and when to open – without getting ambushed by any of those three traps.  
I'll walk you through exactly how it works in Chapters 6 and 7. For now, just know this: when the Compass is working, every decision becomes cleaner. Not easier – the decisions are still yours to make – but cleaner, because you're making them on data and deliberate thought rather than on the hot breath of ego, impatience, or fear.

## **The A-Team Algorithm**

"I'd like to start working four days for five days' pay. Oh yeah, and I want full-time holiday pay too."  
Remember that demand from one of my first team members – someone I thought I was lucky to have because of her experience. I had no clue how to recruit properly, so I just grabbed whoever seemed experienced enough. A pulse helped, too. YOU CANNOT MAKE A BIGGER MISTAKE as a new practice owner.  
Looking back, I realise I approached hiring like people were doing me a favour by accepting my job offer. In reality, with a squat practice, you have a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to build your dream team from scratch. No inherited staff with entrenched bad habits. No toxic personalities who've been there "too long to fire." No one telling you "that's not how we do things here."  
You get to define how you do things here. But only if you hire people who'll help you define it – not people who'll bring someone else's baggage into your brand-new building.  
Most squatpreneurs squander this golden opportunity by using the same outdated recruitment methods corporate dentistry uses. They post a boring job ad listing requirements. They interview people with standard questions. They hire the one who seemed nicest. And then they wonder why, six months in, they're dealing with drama, demands, and disengagement.  
I know. Because that was me.  
The A-Team Algorithm is my playbook for attracting, identifying, and keeping A-grade people. It covers three things corporate dentistry never taught any of us: how to market opportunities instead of posting jobs, how to interview in ways that reveal true character rather than rehearsed answers, and how to build a culture that makes exceptional people want to stay.  
It took me two years and a couple of expensive hiring disasters to begin to figure this out. I'll hand the framework over to you in Chapters 8 through 10, so you don't have to pay my tuition fees.  
For a second, just imagine walking into your practice every morning genuinely excited to see your team. Imagine staff who don't just do their jobs but actively look for ways to make the practice better. Imagine a culture where patients comment on the positive energy the moment they walk through the door.  
That's what a functioning A-Team Algorithm delivers. And the work of building it starts long before you hire anyone – the moment you decide what you actually stand for.

## **The Invisible Waiting List**

"We took about forty or fifty patients in our first month."  
I was proud of this at the time. I even bragged about it a little. I'd opened in a visible location, put up banners, had around a hundred people enquire during the build, and converted enough of them to keep the lights on.  
Now I look at what my Squat Success students are doing and I realise I could have opened with three or four times that number. Without spending a penny more on marketing.  
The Invisible Waiting List is perhaps the most critical element for cash flow in those crucial early months. Most squat practice owners struggle financially because they follow the traditional approach:  
Build the practice …  
Finish the practice …  
Open the doors …  
Start thinking about how to get patients …  
Panic when the bills are coming in faster than the patients!  
It's like planning a party but not inviting people until after you've put the food out. By then, it's too late – everyone's made other plans.  
Here's the psychological reality most squatpreneurs miss: trust doesn't happen on demand. You can't spend thousands on slick ads trying to manufacture trust with people who've never heard of you. That's not how the human brain works.  
What does work is building trust whilst you're building your practice. Sharing the journey. Letting people watch the story unfold. So that by the time you open your doors, you're not a stranger asking for their money – you're someone they already feel like they know.  
I've watched one Squat Success student open with 380 people on his waiting list. Another opened with over 700. A third had to hire an associate within six months because he physically couldn't see the patients who wanted to book.  
None of them paid for a single advert. They just started early.  
This isn't about being an influencer. It isn't about fancy production. It's about being visible and useful to your future patients months before you need them to book. I'll show you exactly how in Chapters 11 through 13.

## **Why These Three Pillars – and Not More**

When I first started mapping what made the difference, I had a longer list. I thought the practice building was a pillar. Or the equipment strategy. Or the financing structure.  
But as I watched more practices open, I noticed something: those other things go wrong all the time – and the practice survives. A dodgy builder adds three months to the timeline? Annoying, expensive, survivable. Equipment you later wish you hadn't bought? Manageable. A financing structure that's tighter than ideal? You grit your teeth and you get through it.  
But get the Certainty Compass wrong – pick the wrong location, the wrong model, the wrong time – and you're fighting the laws of physics from day one.  
Get the A-Team Algorithm wrong – hire the wrong people, build a toxic culture, fail to lead – and you'll wake up dreading Mondays the same way you did as an associate.  
Get the Invisible Waiting List wrong – open with empty books and no community – and the cash flow stress will eat your confidence, your decisions, and eventually your practice.  
These three aren't just important. They're the three that break the whole thing when they go wrong. Everything else is a problem you solve. These are the foundations on which everything else either stands or collapses.

## **How the Three Pillars Work Together**

It's tempting to treat these as three separate projects – tick off the Compass, tick off the Algorithm, tick off the Waiting List, done. In reality, they're interlocking.  
The Certainty Compass tells you *what* to build – the right location, the right model, the right timing. But a perfectly chosen practice with the wrong team will still fail. You need the A-Team Algorithm to bring it to life.  
The A-Team Algorithm tells you *who* to build it with – team members who'll make your patients feel cared for rather than processed. But the best team in the world can't help you if there are no patients walking through the door. You need the Invisible Waiting List to fill those chairs.  
The Invisible Waiting List tells you *how* to get there in time – a community that knows you and trusts you before you open. But if you've picked the wrong location or hired the wrong people, no amount of social media will save you.  
Three pillars. One practice. They hold each other up.  
When I meet dentists whose practices aren't working, I don't ask what's going wrong. I ask which pillar is missing. Usually they can tell me almost immediately. The framework isn't just prescriptive – it's diagnostic. Once you know the three pillars, you can see problems clearly for what they are.  
That's the promise of this book. By the time you finish it, you'll have the framework in your head. You'll spot the traps before you fall into them. You'll build on the foundations rather than gambling on the floor.

**CHAPTER 3**

# **A Tale of Two Dentists: The Fork in the Road**

Let me introduce you to two dentists: Dr. Sarah and Dr. Raj.  
They graduated from the same dental school in the same year. Both were clinically competent, personable, and eager to make their mark. Both started their careers as associates in busy practices, learning the ropes and developing their clinical skills.  
For the first four years, their paths looked remarkably similar from the outside. Both worked in well-established practices, both treated similar patients, both earned comparable salaries. But beneath the surface, they were developing very different perspectives on their profession.  
Sarah saw dentistry primarily as a job – a good job with status and a reliable income, but a job nonetheless. She was conscientious and diligent in her approach to work, focused and efficient, and her principal relied on her to contribute to the practice hitting its targets.  
Raj was similar in many ways, but he saw dentistry as a calling. He took extra courses, stayed late to discuss complex cases with his principal, and constantly thought about how he would do things differently if he were in charge.  
And that's where their paths diverged.  
When they reached that critical 5-year mark as associates – experienced enough to make their next move – Sarah looked at practice ownership and saw nothing but risk. The startup costs. The business headaches. The staffing nightmares. The potential for failure. So she decided to play it safe and move to a corporate dentistry role, which seemed more in line with the career path of her peers in other professions. "Maybe I'll open my own place someday," she told herself, "when the timing's a little better."  
Raj felt those same fears. But he also saw something else: the opportunity to create something of his own. So he took the leap and opened a squat practice.  
Let's check in on them over the years and see how their choices played out.

## **Years 1-2 After the Decision: The Early Divergence**

**Sarah's Path:** Sarah quickly found a position at a well-established corporate dental group. The interview was slick, the practice was modern, and the salary and benefits package looked decent on paper.  
"We're all about patient care, Sarah," the area manager assured her.  
Her first day, she was handed a 45-page employee handbook, three performance targets, and a script for upselling cosmetic treatments. Her appointments were booked at 15-minute intervals.  
"Don't worry," said the practice manager with a tight smile, eerily reading Sarah's mind, "you'll get faster."  
By the end of her first year at the corporate practice, Sarah was bringing home a reliable paycheck. Life was pleasant. She had nice holidays – this year, two weeks in the Maldives, booked months in advance around the practice's busy schedule. Her clinical skills were ... well, they weren't developing much, but she was getting faster at the basics. She had a nagging feeling that she could be doing more comprehensive dentistry, but there simply wasn't time in the schedule.  
"At least it's stable," she thought to herself when she hopped off the 'hamster wheel' for an hour of scrolling through social media during her prescribed lunch break.  
**Raj's Path:** Raj's first year was certainly demanding, as any new business venture would be.  
There were learning curves to navigate. The trial and error of establishing relationships with suppliers that could be relied on. Preparing for CQC inspections. His first hire wasn't the perfect fit. Or the second. So, interviewing skills had to be quickly mastered. Some days were quieter than he'd hoped and he had to learn the hard way that you couldn't just throw money at an ad agency to solve that particular problem!  
And yes, finances required careful attention. Raj kept an eagle eye on his numbers in a way that he'd never had to do as an associate. He created detailed forecasts and tracked his progress meticulously. He made thoughtful decisions about where to invest and where to economise.  
His holiday that year was a long weekend in the Lake District – beautiful, but brief. Nowadays, his 'time off' often included catching up on marketing plans or admin.  
But there were bright spots that made it all worthwhile. The elderly patient who brought homemade biscuits to thank him for taking time to explain her treatment options. The family who drove 45 minutes because they'd heard he was great with nervous children. The freedom to block out a full hour for complex cases that genuinely interested him. The ability to design treatment plans based on what patients truly needed, not what corporate protocols dictated.  
By the end of his first year, Raj was making less than Sarah. He was working longer hours. His car was a year older than he'd planned on keeping it.  
But he also had something else: a practice that was beginning to reflect his values. He'd designed a patient journey that eliminated the typical anxieties of dental visits. His reception area looked more like a comfortable living room than a clinical waiting area. He'd invested in equipment that allowed him to perform treatments that excited him professionally. Most importantly, he was creating the kind of workplace culture he'd always wanted to be part of – one where team members were valued for their contributions, not just their productivity.

## **Years 3-5 After the Decision: The Middle Phase**

**Sarah's Path:** By year three, Sarah had settled into a routine. Clock in, see patients. Clock out, glass of wine and Netflix. The corporate machine hummed along efficiently around her. Her life continued at a pleasant, sedate pace.  
There had been changes, of course. The original area manager was replaced by someone more 'numbers-focused'. The employee handbook grew to 60 pages. The performance targets increased while the appointment times decreased.  
"The company has new investors to satisfy," the new manager explained during another tense staff meeting.  
The turnover among staff was remarkable. Sarah realised one day that she couldn't remember the names of the last three receptionists. The turnover among associate dentists was even higher. Sarah was now the 'senior' associate, having survived just over three years.  
Her salary had increased modestly, but not in proportion to the revenue she generated. When she raised this in her annual review, she was reminded of how she benefited from their 'marketing systems' and 'established patient base'.  
She continued taking nice annual holidays – the sunny Greek Islands called her back year after year – but increasingly she wasn't able to completely switch off and found herself checking work emails daily. And yet another new 'efficiency initiative' meant she faced a backlog of performance reviews upon her return.  
She found herself dreading Mondays. Then Tuesdays. Then ...  
**Raj's Path:** By year three, Raj had found his rhythm. The early chaos had given way to a new era of functioning systems. He'd made plenty of mistakes, but he'd learned from each one.  
The biggest gamechanger had been finding the right practice manager – someone who complemented his skills and shared his vision. Together, they'd built a team that worked.  
The practice wasn't just surviving anymore; it was growing. Raj had expanded his clinical offerings, investing in continuing education that actually interested him rather than whatever would help some faceless name in a corporate head office hit quarterly targets.  
He'd started building a reputation for quality, unhurried care. Patients were referring friends and family. Some were driving considerable distances to see him.  
Financially, things had turned a corner too. Raj was now taking home more than he would have made as an associate. But more importantly, he was building an asset. Every improvement to the practice, every new patient, every positive review was adding value to something he owned.  
That summer, Raj took his first proper two-week holiday since opening the practice – a week in Dubai with his family, staying at a beachfront hotel on The Palm. His team, headed up by his practice manager, handled everything beautifully in his absence. Not a single 'emergency call' interrupted his vacation.  
Was it still hard work? Absolutely. Were there still challenges? Daily. But they were his challenges, his decisions, his practice.

## **A Decade On: The Long-Term Reality**

**Sarah's Path:** A decade in, Sarah barely recognised the idealistic young dentist she'd once been.  
The corporate practice had been acquired, then acquired again. Each transition brought new policies, new targets, new management – but the direction was always the same: more production, less time, higher targets.  
Her clinical skills had stagnated. She could do fillings and crowns with her eyes closed, but more complex procedures were referred out. Yet another 'company policy'.  
She still took her annual holidays, which were needed more than ever now. But she always found herself completely exhausted for the first few days, after months of slogging away on the corporate treadmill. Her colleagues all joked about needing 'a holiday to recover from coming back from holiday'. But no one ever smiled when they said it.  
She lived in a nice semi-detached house in a good area. It was the kind of solid, sensible home that reflected her solid, sensible career choice. She drove a reliable mid-range car, replaced every five years like clockwork.  
The final straw came when the newest corporate owner installed software that they gleefully announced, 'tracks everything'. How long she spent with each patient. How many additional treatments she sold. How her production compared to other associates, in real-time.  
Sarah now made a comfortable living. There had been a steady, close to inflation-matching increase over the last ten years. She had job security, in theory, although the last round of 'restructuring' had seen three long-term associates let go.  
What she didn't have was enthusiasm, growth, or any sense that she was making a difference.  
"Maybe I should look at other corporate practices," she mused from time to time. But deep down, she knew they were all variations on the same theme. And anyway, this one was a pretty short daily commute, so she stayed.  
**Raj's Path:** Ten years in, Raj's practice had evolved into something he was genuinely proud of.  
He now had four associates working with him, all drawn by the practice's ethos and the opportunity to practice comprehensive dentistry. In the top drawer of his desk was a brochure for another practice he was considering acquiring.  
His team was stable and engaged and most had been with him for years. They operated like a well-oiled machine, but one with heart. Birthdays were celebrated. Personal milestones acknowledged. When the head receptionist needed unexpected time off for family reasons, the entire team rallied to cover.  
Patients felt the difference. The practice had won community awards three years running.  
Raj's practice success had translated into personal freedom. He had recently moved his family into a beautiful countryside property. The kind of home he'd never have afforded on an associate's salary. The mortgage would be paid off in just five more years, well ahead of schedule.  
Last year, he'd taken his parents on a month-long trip to visit family back in India – something he'd promised himself he'd do once he 'made it'. Watching his father's face as they visited relatives in style, rather than on a tight budget, had been priceless.  
The practice ran smoothly whether he was there or not. He now worked four days a week, with plans to reduce to three in the coming year. He'd started a scholarship fund for disadvantaged students interested in dentistry. It wasn't going to change the world but being able to pay it forward like this made him smile when he looked in the mirror each morning.  
Financially, Raj was in a different universe from Sarah. Between practice profits and the increasing value of the business itself, his net worth had grown exponentially. He had options.  
Most importantly, he was still growing professionally. He chose continuing education based on his interests, not corporate requirements. He blocked time for complex cases that intellectually stimulated him. He mentored younger dentists.  
He had created something meaningful – a practice that reflected his values, that served his community, that would continue even if he stepped back.  
And he actually looked forward to Mondays.

## **Retirement: The Final Comparison**

### **Sarah's Exit**

After 35 years in dentistry, Sarah's retirement came without fanfare.  
The corporate group she worked for was on its fourth ownership change. The final buyout had brought the predictable "restructuring" – code for letting go of expensive senior associates. They offered her a severance package. She took it.  
Her final day was marked by a hastily organised cake in the staff room. Two people attended. Neither had worked with her for more than eighteen months.  
Her retirement financial position? Comfortable but modest. A pension from years of steady contributions. Savings from consistent income. Enough to live on, not enough to be truly free. She downsized from the family home to a smaller place. Holidays are carefully budgeted now. The dream of the villa in Spain didn't materialise.  
But the financial situation isn't what haunts her. It's the legacy question.  
Her children asked her once: "Mum, what did you achieve in your career?"  
She struggled to answer.  
She'd treated thousands of patients, sure. But she couldn't name many. The corporate machine had made her efficient but disconnected. She'd been a skilled technician in someone else's system. No practice bearing her name. No team she'd built. No community impact she could point to. Just three and a half decades of showing up, treating teeth, going home.  
The corporate machine that had employed her replaced her within a week. Patients were assigned to the newest associate. Life at the practice continued as if she'd never been there.  
Sarah's biggest regret? "I wish I'd taken the risk when I was younger. When I had the energy and time to build something meaningful. I chose safety and got ... this."

### **Raj's Exit**

Raj's retirement was entirely on his terms.  
After 32 years of practice ownership, he received an offer he couldn't refuse. The practice he'd built – grown to four associates, known throughout the community, genuinely loved by patients and staff – sold for £2.8 million.  
Combined with the commercial property he'd been able to buy ten years in (because practice cash flow made it possible), his net worth at retirement exceeded £4 million.  
But the money isn't the real story.  
Raj's "retirement" wasn't really retirement in the traditional sense. Three months travelling India with his family, reconnecting with relatives. Then volunteer work with a dental charity in Africa, sharing his skills where they were genuinely needed. Then consulting with young dentists who wanted to open practices of their own. Then a seat on the board of his dental school.  
His legacy was visible everywhere. Four of his associates had gone on to open their own practices, mentored by Raj along the way. The practice he built still carried his philosophy and values under new ownership. Patients still asked about him years later. Team members invited him to weddings and family celebrations.  
At his retirement party – which he organised himself because he wanted to thank everyone personally – over 200 people attended. Team members from twenty years ago. Patients who'd been with him from the beginning. Local business owners. Community leaders. They didn't come because they had to. They came because he'd built something they'd been part of.  
When asked about his career, Raj says: "I got to practise dentistry exactly how I believed it should be practised. I built a team I loved working with. I made a difference in my community. And I created financial freedom for my family. What more could I ask?"  
Beyond the financial freedom, Raj had something perhaps even more valuable: the satisfaction of knowing he'd built something meaningful. Something that reflected his values. Something that would outlast him.

**CHAPTER 4**

# **The Reality Check**

So here we are. You've read my journey from nearly getting kicked out of school to building a thriving dental practice. You've seen how Dr. Sarah and Dr. Raj's paths diverged. You're probably feeling that tingle of excitement thinking, "I could do this!"  
And maybe you can.  
But before we dive headfirst into building your dream practice, we need to have what I call the 'tea and rusk' conversation – the kind where someone who cares about you sits you down with a cup of tea and tells you the straight truth.  
Because here's what I know: Opening a squat practice isn't just about clinical skills or even about having a dream. It's about having the right financial foundation, mindset, and commitment to see it through.  
Let's start with the question I get asked all the time – and the one that's actually the wrong question.

## **The Question Everyone Asks (And Why It's Wrong)**

"Bobby, how much does it cost to set up a practice?"  
If I had a pound for every time I've heard this question, I could probably open another practice (with enough left for a very long holiday somewhere warm). But here's the thing – it's the wrong question.  
The right question is: "How much does it cost to RUN a practice?"  
Let me share something I learned that I think is important for you to understand right now. Back in the early days when I was listening to podcasts every day on my way to work as an associate, I came across a business saying that's stuck with me ever since: "revenue is vanity, profit is reality, and cash flow is sanity."  
At the time, I was too busy drilling and filling to fully grasp what he meant. But when I finally got into running my own practice, those words became wisdom.  
I discovered that revenue – no matter how high – is meaningless if it doesn't translate into profit. And profit is just an illusion until it turns into cold hard cash in the bank. You could be turning over £100,000 per month but with bills to pay, you could find yourself on your knees in the bank manager's office begging for money to make payroll in two days.  
The saying was right. The headline numbers can look impressive, but it's what's left over after all the bills are paid that determines the success of your practice. And it's the timing of money coming in versus money going out that keeps the lights on.

## **The True Financial Requirements**

Let's get real about the numbers. You need two distinct types of capital:

### **1. Startup Capital**

You need £80,000-£100,000 in liquid assets, minimum. Full stop.  
If you don't have this, you need to pause and figure out why. And this isn't meant to be harsh – it's meant to protect you. If you haven't been able to save this amount on a dentist's salary, we need to ask some honest questions:

* Are your clinical skills generating sufficient income?  
* Do you need to invest in advanced training first?  
* Is there something about your relationship with money that needs addressing?

This final point deserves special attention. Many people have what I call a 'broken financial thermostat'. They grew up hearing things like 'money doesn't grow on trees' or 'rich people are greedy' or 'money is the root of all evil'. Heck, you may even have turned up the bass on your iPhone on the way to lectures as a student listening to Puff Daddy rapping 'bout Mo Money, Mo Problems – lol. Or some people have been exposed to people around them with 'bad money habits'. These experiences develop into deep-seated beliefs, creating a subconscious ceiling on what they think they deserve to earn or accumulate. If you're constantly spending everything you make, always finding yourself short despite a high income, or feel guilty about wanting financial success, you might have some money mindset work to do before taking on practice ownership.

### **2. Working Capital and Cashflow Reality**

This is the part that catches most dentists off guard. A typical squat practice will have monthly running costs of £12,000-£20,000 from day one.  
Shocked? Well, that's just from day one. The practice you are currently working at may be many times that. Maybe that explains why the practice owner isn't always smiling first thing in the morning or seems to be overconcerned about gaps in the diary. Maybe?  
Let's break this down:

* Loan repayments  
* Equipment leases  
* Staff salaries  
* Utilities and rent  
* Supplies and inventory  
* Insurance  
* Marketing  
* Professional fees

And here's the kicker – most of these costs are what accountants call 'fixed costs'. These costs exist whether you see zero patients or fifty patients. They're due on the first of the month, regardless.

### **The Money Mindset Problem**

Now, let me share something that might make you uncomfortable: Most dentists are financially illiterate when it comes to business.  
I know because I was one of them.  
When I ask my workshop delegates to tell me how much money leaves their account each month, nine out of ten can't answer. They don't have a spreadsheet. They don't budget. They just have 'enough' coming in to cover whatever they spend.  
This works fine when you're an associate with a steady paycheck. It's a disaster when you're running a business.  
Why? Because most dentists develop what I call 'High Income Comfort Syndrome'. From being one of the highest-paid graduates straight out of university, we get used to money just 'being there'. We never have to think too hard about it.  
But business ownership requires a completely different relationship with money. You need to understand:

* Fixed vs variable costs ( ... hey, we just talked about that, so you're already one step ahead of most people!)  
* Gross vs net margin  
* Cash flow forecasting  
* Break-even analysis  
* Return on investment

Don't worry, I'm not going to turn this into an accounting textbook. But you do need to get comfortable with these concepts. They're not rocket science, but they are the language of business survival.

## **The Self-Assessment: Are You Really Ready?**

Let's get brutally honest. Answer these questions:

### **1. Clinical Skills Assessment**

* Can you perform high-value procedures (implants, smile makeovers, complex restorative work)?  
* Do these procedures genuinely excite you, or are you just chasing the money?  
* Are you committed to ongoing clinical education?

If you answered no to the first question, you need to either develop these skills first or plan on hiring an associate from day one. But beware – associates typically take 40-50% of gross, which will seriously dent your profit margins.  
Let me be blunt: Starting with an associate from day one is usually a recipe for financial disaster. Here's why:  
You've already got £12-20k in monthly fixed costs. Now add an associate taking 45% of everything they produce. That means you need to generate significantly more revenue just to break even. And remember – you'll need exceptional marketing to drive that volume of patients from day one. You're not just feeding your own practice now; you're feeding two hungry mouths. The financial dynamics change dramatically, and not in your favour.  
I've seen too many dentists try this route and end up working harder for less money than they made as associates for way too long. Don't fall into this trap.

### **2. Financial Fitness Test**

* Do you know exactly how much you spend each month?  
* Have you ever created and stuck to a budget?  
* Can you save at least 20% of your current income?  
* Do you have any personal debt (excluding mortgage)?  
* Are you prepared to potentially earn less for 1-2 years?

If you answered 'no' to more than two of these, you need to work on your financial fitness before taking on practice ownership.  
Here's your homework – starting TODAY:

* Download a budgeting app and track every single penny you spend for the next 30 days  
* Create a simple spreadsheet showing your monthly income and expenses  
* Set up an automatic transfer to save 20% of your next paycheque  
* Cancel any unnecessary subscriptions or memberships  
* Start educating yourself – pick up a basic business finance book and commit to reading 10 pages daily

These aren't just suggestions. They're your first test. If you can't commit to these simple steps, you're not ready for practice ownership.

### **3. Mindset Reality Check**

* Can you handle making decisions with incomplete information?  
* Are you willing to have difficult conversations with staff, patients, and suppliers?  
* Can you push through when things get tough (and they will)?  
* Are you prepared to work longer hours than you do now?  
* Can you learn from failures without being crushed by them?

Here's your mindset homework:

* Start a reflection journal tonight. Write about a recent challenging situation and how you handled it  
* Identify one difficult conversation you've been avoiding and schedule it for next week  
* Make one decision this week with only 70% of the information you'd like to have  
* Ask three people to give you honest feedback about your resilience and adaptability  
* Set yourself a challenge outside your comfort zone – and stick to it for 30 days

These exercises will reveal your current mindset gaps and start building the mental muscles you'll need as a practice owner.

### **4. The Support System Question**

Do you have people in your life who will support you through this journey? Because let me tell you – there will be days when you'll need someone to remind you why you started.  
My wife was that person for me. When I wanted to give up, she saw something in me that I couldn't see in myself. Who's that person for you?  
Your support system homework:

* Make a list of five people who genuinely believe in you  
* Have an honest conversation with your partner/spouse about your practice ownership dream

Remember: You can't do this alone. Building your support network starts now, not when you open your doors.

## **The Three Crisis Points You Must Prepare For**

Through my own experience and guiding other squatpreneurs, I've identified three crisis points that test everyone's resilience:

### **1. The Building Crisis**

Your construction will cost 10-15% more than budgeted. Something unexpected will happen. Your builder might let you down. This is almost inevitable.  
Can you handle that financially? And just as importantly, emotionally?

### **2. The Marketing Panic**

There will be a point where you're not getting enough patient inquiries. The silence will be deafening. Your mind will spiral: "What if nobody comes?"  
Will you have the nerve to stay the course with your marketing strategy rather than panic-spending on ineffective advertising?

### **3. The Team Turnover**

Your first hire probably won't work out. Neither might your second. Building the right team takes time.  
Can you handle the stress of recruitment while running a practice?  
These three crises are near-certainties. In the next chapter I'll walk you through the psychological techniques for facing them when they hit. For now, your job is to make sure you're prepared practically:

* Build a 10-15% contingency into your construction budget from day one  
* Set aside a 'break glass in emergency' fund separate from your working capital  
* Design a marketing plan that doesn't rely on any single channel – so if one goes quiet, the others still work  
* Identify one or two mentors you can actually call when a hiring decision goes wrong  
* Know the suppliers, professionals, and peers you'd lean on in each scenario before you need them

Remember: the goal isn't to expect failure, but to be so prepared that no challenge can knock you off course.

## **The Qualification Tool: Your Decision Matrix**

Still with me? Good. Now let's use this simple tool to determine if you're ready:  
Essential Requirements (must have all):

* £80,000-£100,000 in liquid assets  
* Strong clinical skills in high-value procedures  
* Basic understanding of business financials  
* An emotional support system in place  
* Willingness to work 50+ hours per week initially

Important Factors (need at least 3):

* Previous business experience (any kind)  
* A mentor or advisor who has already taken the path you're on  
* Established patient following  
* Marketing knowledge or willingness to learn  
* Leadership experience (even informal)

Red Flags (more than 2 means reconsider):

* Significant personal debt  
* Inability to save consistently  
* Resistance to learning business skills  
* Expectation of immediate high income  
* History of giving up when things get tough

## **The Growth Mindset Requirement**

Here's the final piece of the puzzle: You must have what psychologists call a 'growth mindset'.  
When I was an associate, I consumed everything I could about business. Podcasts during my commute instead of listening to local radio banter. And you'd always find me with my head in a book on weekends. I found all this 'business stuff' genuinely interesting.  
If the thought of learning about business makes you want to take a nap, practice ownership might not be for you. And that's okay! The world needs excellent associate dentists too.  
But if you're thinking, "I may not know this stuff now, but I can learn it," then you've got the mindset that matters.

# **Making Decisions That Don’t Backfire**

## **The Three Infections, Up Close**

Back in Chapter 2, I introduced you to the three decision-making infections that sabotage squat practices: the Prestige Virus, Quick-Fix Fever, and Analysis Paralysis Syndrome. Now it's time to look at each one properly – because recognising them in yourself is the first step in the Certainty Compass framework, and the difference between a thriving practice and an expensive cautionary tale often comes down to which of the three you caught, and when.  
The difference between dentists who thrive and dentists who struggle isn't intelligence, clinical skill, or available capital. I've watched dozens of practice owners make these decisions, and the pattern is always the same. The difference is the quality of the decision-making process – and specifically, whether one of these three infections got there first.  
Let's drag each one into the light.

### **Infection #1: The Prestige Virus**

Symptoms: Obsessing over premium equipment, prestigious locations, and what will impress peers  
This is the infection I nearly caught with that dental chair. It's also the reason I bought an expensive car when I was a young associate – not because I loved cars, but because I wanted to fit in with the other dentists I knew.  
The Prestige Virus makes you confuse appearance with substance. It whispers: "What will people think? What will other dentists say? How will this look on Instagram?"  
Let me tell you about Amir. Brilliant clinician, seven years qualified, excellent income. When he started planning his squat, he became fixated on a specific location – a prestigious street in an affluent area with high foot traffic and excellent visibility.  
"It's perfect," he told me during our consultation. "Right next to the high-end boutiques, near the private members' club. Everyone will see it."  
"Have you checked the demographics?" I asked.  
"Well, it's obviously an affluent area," he shot back.  
"But have you actually checked? Population density? Age distribution? How many practices already serve that area?"  
He hadn't. Because he'd already decided based on prestige, not data.  
When we did the analysis together, we discovered that area was already served by four established practices and had a population that skewed older and lower-income (great for dentures, not ideal for his cosmetic focus). The rental costs were triple what he'd pay elsewhere.  
Six months later, Amir opened in a different location – less prestigious, better demographics, one-third the rent. His practice is thriving. The 'perfect' prestigious location? Still vacant nearly two years later.

#### **How the Prestige Virus spreads:**

* Comparing yourself to other dentists on social media  
* Focusing on what looks good rather than what works well  
* Making decisions to impress colleagues rather than serve patients  
* Prioritising brand names over functionality

The cure: Ask yourself one simple question before every major purchase or decision: "Am I choosing this because it's the best solution, or because I want to be seen choosing it?"

### **Infection #2: The Quick-Fix Fever**

Symptoms: Rushing research, trusting gut over data, making decisions to escape current circumstances  
This infection is particularly dangerous because it disguises itself as decisiveness. You're not being hasty – you're being 'action-oriented'. You're not skipping important steps – you're 'moving fast'.  
Quick-Fix Fever usually strikes when you're desperate to escape your current situation. Maybe you're miserable in corporate dentistry. Maybe you've been planning for so long you just want to get started. Maybe you're worried that if you don't act now, you'll never act.  
I caught this infection badly when choosing my first builder. I'd interviewed three companies. Two seemed professional but expensive. The third was cheaper and available immediately.  
I ignored the warning signs:

* They couldn't provide recent references  
* Their quote was suspiciously low  
* They seemed evasive about timelines  
* My gut told me something was off

But I was in a hurry. I'd already quit my associate position. The lease was signed. I needed to get started.  
You know what happened next. Fraudulent VAT charges. Half-built walls with no roof. Six months of delays and thousands in additional costs.  
That 'quick' decision ended up being the slowest, most expensive option possible.  
Sanjay's story is even more dramatic. He found what he thought was the perfect building for his practice. Great location, good price, available immediately. He signed a five-year lease after one viewing.  
Only later did he discover:

* Previous tenant left due to structural issues  
* Planning permission for dental use was complicated  
* The landlord had a history of disputes with tenants

He managed to break the lease, but it cost him £20,000 and eight months of stress.

#### **How Quick-Fix Fever spreads:**

* Pressure to make a decision 'now or never'  
* Emotional urgency to escape current situation  
* Impatience after lengthy planning  
* Fear that opportunities will disappear

The cure: Implement a mandatory 48-hour pause before any decision over £5,000. Use that time to gather data, check references, and let the emotional urgency settle.

### **Infection #3: Analysis Paralysis Syndrome**

Symptoms: Overthinking until opportunities pass, requiring certainty before action, paralysis disguised as thoroughness  
If Quick-Fix Fever makes you act too fast, Analysis Paralysis Syndrome stops you from acting at all.  
This infection is sneaky because it feels responsible. You're not being indecisive – you're being 'thorough'. You're not procrastinating – you're 'doing your due diligence'.  
I see this most often with location selection. The dentist creates elaborate spreadsheets comparing dozens of potential locations. They analyse demographic data down to the smallest detail. They visit each area multiple times at different times of day.  
All of this is good practice. The problem comes when it never ends.  
Meet Meera. She's been 'researching locations' for three years. She has a spreadsheet with 47 potential areas; each scored across 23 different criteria. She's visited 31 of them.  
She still hasn't made a decision.  
"I just want to be sure," she tells me. "This is such an important decision, Bobby."  
She's right – it is important. But she's waiting for a level of certainty that doesn't exist. There's no location that scores 100% across all criteria. Every choice involves trade-offs.  
Meanwhile, three of the 'good' locations she identified two years ago now have new practices. The opportunities passed while she was analysing.  
Analysis Paralysis often stems from fear of making the wrong choice. But here's the truth: not choosing is also a choice. And it's usually the worst choice of all.

#### **How Analysis Paralysis spreads:**

* Fear of making mistakes  
* Perfectionist tendencies  
* Treating research as action  
* Waiting for complete certainty before moving

The cure: Set decision deadlines. "I will make this decision by [specific date] with whatever information I have by then."

## **The Triple-Check Treatment: Your Antidote to Bad Decisions**

Now that you can recognise these infections, let's talk about the cure.  
The Triple-Check Treatment is a three-question framework that I use for every major decision in my practice. It's simple, but it's powerful enough to prevent most decision-making disasters.  
Before making any significant decision (location, equipment, staff, timing), run it through these three questions:

### **Question 1: "What's the Patient Benefit?"**

This question is your antidote to the Prestige Virus.  
It forces you to shift focus from "how does this make ME look?" to "how does this serve my PATIENTS?"  
Let's go back to that £30,000 dental chair. When my mentor asked me to articulate the patient benefit, I couldn't. The £15,000 chair would provide identical patient comfort and clinical functionality.  
The extra £15,000 was buying prestige, not patient benefit.  
Here's how to apply this question:  
For location decisions: "Will patients actually benefit from this prestigious address, or am I just paying for status?"  
Consider: accessibility, parking, proximity to target demographic. A less prestigious location with better parking and easier access might serve patients better.  
For equipment purchases: "Will this equipment actually improve patient outcomes or experience?"  
That fancy 3D scanner might be impressive, but if you're not doing the procedures that require it, it's nothing more than an expensive ornament.  
For practice design: "Will patients appreciate this design choice, or am I just pleasing my own aesthetic preferences?"  
Marble reception desks look impressive, but patients might value comfortable seating and short wait times more.

### **Question 2: "What's the Data Saying?"**

This question is your antidote to Quick-Fix Fever.  
It forces you to slow down and base decisions on evidence rather than emotion or urgency.  
When Amir fell in love with that prestigious location, this question would have revealed:

* Four competing practices in the immediate area  
* Population density lower than optimal  
* Demographics misaligned with his services  
* Rental costs 3x comparable locations

The data was screaming 'wrong choice'. But without asking the question, he almost didn't hear it.  
Here's how to apply this question:  
For location decisions, check:

* Population density and demographics  
* Competitor analysis (practice-to-population ratio)  
* Economic indicators for the area  
* Transport links and accessibility  
* Historical data on similar practices in the area

For financial decisions, examine:

* Market rates for similar purchases  
* Historical data on ROI  
* Comparable case studies  
* Expert opinion from accountants/brokers

For timing decisions, review:

* Market conditions and trends  
* Personal readiness metrics  
* Financial position vs. requirements  
* Seasonal factors affecting dental demand

### **Question 3: "What's the Worst-Case Scenario (and can I handle it)?"**

This question is your antidote to Analysis Paralysis Syndrome.  
It doesn't eliminate risk – it makes risk concrete and manageable instead of vague and terrifying.  
When Meera was paralysed by location choice, I asked her: "What's the worst-case scenario if you choose Location A and it doesn't work out?"  
She thought about it. "I suppose ... I'd have a lease I couldn't break, I'd lose my initial investment, patients wouldn't come ..."  
"Okay," I said. "How would you handle that?"  
"Well, I could return to associate work. Pay off debts over 3-4 years. Try again later with more knowledge."  
"So worst-case scenario is you're back where you are now, but 3-4 years delayed and with valuable learning. Is that survivable?"  
"Actually ... yes. I'd survive that."  
"Right. So, the worst-case scenario isn't catastrophic failure and financial ruin. It's a temporary setback. Now, what's the worst-case scenario if you DON'T make a decision?"  
"I stay stuck as an associate, getting more frustrated, watching opportunities pass ..."  
"For how long?"  
"Could be years. Could be forever."  
That conversation helped her realise that the 'safe' choice of not deciding was actually the riskier option long-term.  
Here's how to apply this question:  
Make fears concrete: Instead of vague anxiety, write down specific worst-case outcomes. What actually happens if this decision fails?  
Develop recovery plans: For each worst-case scenario, create a specific plan. How would you recover? What resources would you need?  
Assess probability: With concrete scenarios, you can assess realistic probability. Is this worst-case 50% likely or 5% likely?  
Set decision triggers: If worst-case scenario happens, what specific indicators will tell you? When will you activate your recovery plan?

## **Common Decision Traps (And How to Avoid Them)**

Beyond the three infections, watch for these specific decision traps:

#### **The Sunk Cost Fallacy**

"I've already spent £10,000 on this plan, so I can't change course now."  
Reality: Money already spent is gone regardless. Base your decision on future value, not past investment.

#### **The Shiny Object Syndrome**

"That new technology/system/approach sounds amazing – I should completely change my plan!"  
Reality: New information should refine your plan, not constantly restart it. Use the Triple-Check before changing course.

#### **The Herd Mentality**

"Everyone's doing it this way, so it must be right."  
Reality: What works for others may not work for you. Use data about your specific situation.

#### **The Expert Worship**

"This person is successful, so I should do exactly what they did."  
Reality: Success leaves clues, not blueprints. Learn principles, don't copy actions.

## **Decision 1: Which Business Model?**

"I'm going to open a general dental practice."  
When I hear this, I always ask: "Why?"  
Usually I get: "Well ... that's what a dental practice is, isn't it?"  
No. A practice can be whatever you design it to be. The question isn't "what's normal?" It's "what's optimal for MY skills, MY market, and MY lifestyle?"  
Let me show you what I mean with three quick examples:  
Maya wanted to go fully private because 'that's where the money is'. But she loved variety – treating families, building relationships. We analysed her skills: good at everything, not exceptional at any one thing. Her model? Mixed practice – 60% NHS foundation, 40% private. This gave her steady patient flow from day one and the relationship-based practice she craved. Several years later, she's gradually shifted to 70% private, but that NHS foundation gave her the runway she needed.  
James had extensive implant training and stunning Instagram results. He wanted to go specialist-only. But the maths didn't work. At £3,000 per case, he needed 15-20 implant cases monthly to thrive. From day one? Impossible. We restructured: 70% specialist focus, 30% general dentistry for cash flow stability. Within 18 months he hit his target. Within three years, he was 90% specialist. But he would have gone bankrupt in year one pursuing purely specialist from the start.  
Aisha wanted a standard general practice. But when we analysed her clinical strengths, a pattern emerged – she kept talking about the sleep apnoea patients she was quietly treating. She was fitting 4-5 mandibular advancement devices a month without even marketing the service. Market research showed zero competitors in her area offering this. We repositioned: general practice with a dental sleep medicine specialty. Three years later, sleep medicine accounts for 35% of her revenue. She's the regional expert. And she never would have discovered this without systematic analysis.  
The lesson? Your business model must match your ACTUAL clinical capabilities and genuine interests, not your aspirational fantasy or what seems 'normal'.

## **The Three-Question Framework**

To choose your model, answer these honestly:

### **1. What can you confidently do RIGHT NOW?**

Create three lists:

* **Confident and Current:** Procedures you could do in your sleep  
* **Competent but Rusty:** Skills you have but need refreshing  
* **Trained but Not Ready:** Courses you've done but lack clinical confidence

When I did this, my 'confident' list had restorative work and aesthetics. On my 'trained but not ready' list was complex implants and full arch work -I had placed around 300 implants but to build the practice I saw myself building I still needed to push myself to the next level.

### **2. What revenue do you actually need?**

If you need £25,000 monthly revenue, work backwards:

* **High-volume model:** 100+ patients at £150-250 each (Can you handle this pace?)  
* **Medium-volume model:** 50-60 patients at £400-600 each (The sweet spot for most)  
* **Low-volume model:** 10-15 patients at £2,000+ each (Requires specialist skills + marketing)

Each model needs different skills – not just clinical, but business skills too.

### **3. What do you actually ENJOY doing?**

Imagine a typical day in each model. Which energizes you? Which exhausts you? I discovered I wanted variety and time with patients – ruling out both high-volume rush and low-volume repetition. Medium-volume was my sweet spot.  
Match all three answers, and your model becomes clear.

## **The Three-Question Location Framework**

### **1. Does your target patient avatar actually live/work here?**

Define your avatar first (age, income, values, needs). Then check if they're actually present in meaningful numbers. Don't guess – pull actual census data.  
For a general practice, you typically need 30,000+ population within 3 miles, with 20-25% in your target age range. For specialist practices, you might need larger geographic areas but lower percentages.

### **2. What do the competition numbers really say?**

Calculate practice-to-population ratio. But also, mystery shop competitors. Sometimes an "oversaturated" area is just full of mediocre practices. That's opportunity.  
I thought Leamington might be competitive with twelve practices. But when I mystery shopped them, I discovered they were all NHS-focused with 3-4 week waiting times. The market was starving for a modern private practice.  
Don't just count competitors. Assess their quality. Check their websites. Call and try to book. Read their reviews. Look for gaps in service, hours, or patient experience.

### **3. Can patients actually GET to you?**

Parking matters more than prestige. Public transport matters. Visibility matters less than most dentists think.  
I've seen practices fail in gorgeous high-street locations because patients couldn't park. I've seen practices thrive in less visible locations because they had convenient parking and easy access.  
Spend time at potential locations during your planned hours. Tuesday at 10 AM. Thursday at 5 PM. Saturday at 9 AM. Watch the traffic. See where people park. Notice who's around.  
Your fantasy location might be dead when you need patients.

### **Calculate True Occupancy Costs:**

Rent is just the start. Add service charges, business rates, insurance, utilities, maintenance. Then ask: what percentage of projected revenue is this?  
Target: 8-12% of revenue. If you're at 15-20%, your margins will be razor-thin.  
Run every potential location through these questions with actual data, not feelings. I almost chose a prestigious location that would have bankrupted me. The Leamington property seemed 'not ideal' but the data was perfect.  
Data saved me from a costly mistake. It can save you too.

Notice these aren't vague promises. They're specific, tangible elements of the experience.

## **The Power of Social Proof**

Notice in my 'After' example, I included a quote from an existing nurse: "I got tired of office politics and being treated like a number. Here, it feels like I'm part of a family."

Yes, she did. And that story demonstrated something money can't buy – that people valued the culture enough to sacrifice short-term compensation.  
Social proof in recruitment works the same way it does in patient marketing. Third-party validation is more credible than anything you say about yourself.

Authenticity matters more than perfection.

## **The Salary Question: When and How**

Notice in my 'After' example, I mentioned salary in the P.S., not the main body.  
This was deliberate.  
Leading with salary sends a message: this is primarily a financial transaction. Following with salary sends a different message: compensation matters, but it's not the most important thing.  
Here's my framework for addressing salary in opportunity marketing:  
DON'T lead with salary. Don't make it the headline or first thing you mention.  
DO acknowledge it exists. Don't pretend money doesn't matter. It does.  
DO position it appropriately. 'Competitive salary' is meaningless. "We pay above market rate because we want the best people" says something different.  
DO make it secondary to opportunity. The opportunity should be compelling enough that people would consider it even at market rate. Above-market compensation is then the cherry on top.  
In my recruitment marketing, I typically say something like:  
"We offer competitive compensation that reflects the value we place on exceptional team members. But more importantly, we're not just trading money for your time – we're offering the chance to be part of creating something special."  
This frames compensation as important but not primary. And it attracts people who think the same way.

## **What Better Hiring Actually Looks Like**

After my hiring disasters, I put as much effort into getting proficient at recruitment as I had into any dental skill I'd mastered.  
The starting point was obvious: I needed interview methods that actually reveal character rather than just test memory.  
The approach that changed everything for me was experiential interviewing.  
The idea is simple but powerful. Instead of asking candidates to TELL you about their past behaviour, you create situations that REVEAL their actual character. Stop asking people to describe themselves – set up moments that make them show you who they are.  
Let me give you an example of how this plays out in practice.

## **The Unusual Request Test: The 6:55 AM Interview**

When I needed to hire a new receptionist, I'd learnt my lesson. I wasn't going to rely on polished answers to rehearsed questions.  
Instead, I asked the shortlisted candidates to come for an interview at 6:55 AM.  
Yes, you read that right. Five minutes before 7 AM.  
The responses to this request alone were revealing:  
Candidate A: "Oh, that's quite early! Is there any flexibility on the time?" (Translation: I'm not that interested in this opportunity)  
Candidate B: "No problem at all. I'll be there." (Translation: I'm adaptable and willing)  
Candidate C: "That's an unusual time! Can I ask why?" (Translation: I'm curious and think independently)  
I invited Candidates B and C. Candidate A removed herself from consideration by her response.  
On interview day, I observed everything from the moment they arrived:  
Candidate B: Arrived at 6:50 AM (early, showing respect for timing), dressed professionally despite the ridiculous hour, bright and alert, smiled warmly when greeting me.  
Candidate C: Arrived at 7:02 AM (slightly late, but texted ahead to explain traffic), dressed appropriately, looked a bit tired but was making an effort to be present and engaged.  
During the "interview," I didn't ask standard questions. Instead, I said:  
"I know 6:55 AM is an unreasonable time for an interview. Can you tell me honestly – what was your reaction when I suggested it?"  
Candidate B: "I thought it was unusual! But I figured there must be a reason, and I wanted to show I'm flexible. Plus, it tells me you're not a typical practice, which is exactly what attracted me to the role."  
Candidate C: "To be honest, I was a bit annoyed at first. But then I thought – if I'm going to work somewhere, I need to know they think differently. And arriving on time for a 6:55 AM interview is way easier than dealing with a difficult patient at 5 PM on a Friday."  
Both excellent answers. Both revealed something important: adaptability, self-awareness, and ability to reframe challenges.  
But the 6:55 AM interview wasn't about the time. It was about observing:

* **Reliability:** Did they show up when they said they would?  
* **Adaptability:** How did they handle an unusual request?  
* **Attitude:** Were they resentful or accepting?  
* **Professionalism:** Did they maintain standards despite the inconvenience?

A question for you here ... given those 3 candidates, their behaviour and responses, who would you have hired?  
I went with Candidate B. That early morning interview told me everything I needed to know about her character. And she didn't let me down.  
Here are other experiential techniques I use alongside the unusual-request test. Each one creates a situation mimicking a real-life scenario the candidate might face in a dental business – and each reveals more than any scripted question could.

## **The Observation Opportunity**

Create a situation where you can observe candidates when they think they're not being evaluated.  
Some possibilities:  
The Interrupted Interview: Excuse yourself for an "urgent call" halfway through. Before leaving, invite them to look around the space. Then observe (subtly) what they actually do.  
Do they:

* Sit patiently and wait?  
* Look around with genuine interest?  
* Check their phone the entire time?  
* Engage with any team members they encounter?  
* Snoop inappropriately?

When you return, ask: "What did you notice while I was gone?"  
Their observations (or lack thereof) will reveal a lot about initiative, curiosity, respect for boundaries, and genuine interest in your practice.  
The Practical Problem Test: Instead of asking "How would you handle X?" – that's what everyone does – why not actually role-play the situation?  
For a dental nurse: "A patient calls in severe pain, we're fully booked, they're upset. You can't reach me. Show me how you'd handle this."  
Then watch how they:

* Stay calm under pressure (or don't)  
* Show empathy (genuine or scripted)  
* Problem-solve creatively (or freeze)  
* Take ownership (or defer responsibility)  
* Communicate under stress

This reveals actual behaviour, not rehearsed stories about past behaviour.

## **More Techniques in the Toolkit**

This part of the job now genuinely excites me – whereas before I used to dread interview days. Here are more techniques in the toolkit:  
The Early Arrival Observation: Ask candidates to arrive 30 minutes early and spend time in the reception area observing the practice in action. Then start the interview by asking: "What did you notice about how we operate?"  
This reveals observation skills, genuine interest, and whether they're actually paying attention to your culture or just going through interview motions.  
The Team Member Interaction: Have candidates spend 10 minutes with a current team member (not you) before the formal interview. The team member isn't interviewing them. They're just showing them around, answering questions.  
Afterwards, you get feedback from your team member about the interaction. Were they respectful? Curious? Engaged? Did they treat your team member as important or just as an obstacle to getting to you?  
How candidates treat people they perceive as "less important" reveals more about character than how they treat the boss.  
The Unexpected Scenario: Partway through the interview, introduce something unexpected. A "patient" (actually a team member) interrupts with an urgent question. A phone rings. Something happens that breaks the interview flow.  
How do candidates handle this? Do they get flustered? Roll with it? Show patience? Get annoyed?  
Real work is full of interruptions and unexpected situations. This reveals how they'll actually handle daily practice life.  
The Critique Request: Near the end of the interview, show them something about your practice (your website, a patient information leaflet, your waiting room setup) and ask: "If you could improve one thing about this, what would it be?"  
This will test multiple things:

* Do they give honest, constructive feedback?  
* Can they be diplomatic whilst being direct?  
* Do they notice details and think critically?  
* Or do they default to "everything's perfect"?

The Working Interview: For key positions, consider inviting finalists to spend 2-3 hours actually working in the practice (paid, of course). Not a full day – just long enough to see them in action.  
You'll learn more in those 2-3 hours than in a dozen traditional interviews.  
The principle behind all of these: stop asking people to tell you who they are. Create situations where they SHOW you who they are.  
You don't need to use all of them. Pick two or three that fit your practice and your style, and build from there. The point is this: you're done relying on traditional interviews that clearly don't work.  
Next up, another huge lesson I learned.

## **Testing for Culture Fit: Beyond "Do I Like Them?"**

Here's another mistake I made early on: I confused "liking someone" with "culture fit."  
I liked my first hire. She seemed nice in the interview. We had good conversation. I thought she'd fit in great.  
She didn't. Because I'd never actually defined what "culture fit" meant beyond "seems pleasant."  
Here's the more systematic approach I use now. Before any interview process, I define 3-4 non-negotiable values for the role – the things I absolutely won't compromise on, whoever the candidate is. For my practice, those were:  
Patient care over profit – We do what's right for patients, even when it costs us  
Team over individual – We support each other's success  
Growth mindset – We see challenges as learning opportunities  
Authentic communication – We say what we mean with kindness  
The question then is: how do you TEST for these values in an interview?  
Not by asking "Do you value patient care?" Everyone says yes to that.  
Instead, create scenarios that force the candidate to show you how they actually think:  
Testing "Patient Care Over Profit": "A long-standing patient comes in with pain. Examining them, you realise they need a root canal followed by a crown – roughly £1,500 of work. They break down during the consultation and tell you they've just lost their job and can't afford anything right now. What do you do?"  
Listen for whether they treat this as a business problem (how do we still close the sale?) or a human one (how do we get this person out of pain and into a plan that works for their actual life?). The best candidates will think about staged treatment, payment plans, interim solutions for the pain, and the relationship with the patient – not just the treatment they were going to bill for.  
Testing "Team Over Individual": "It's 5:30, you're supposed to finish. You have plans. A colleague is drowning in admin after a difficult day. What do you do?"  
Listen for flexibility balanced with boundaries (martyrs who never say no aren't healthy either).  
Testing "Growth Mindset": "Tell me about a mistake you made. What did you learn?"  
Listen for ownership vs. blame. Learning vs. defensiveness. Growth vs. fixed thinking.  
Testing "Authentic Communication": "What's one thing about our practice you'd do differently?"  
This tests whether they're honest enough to share constructive feedback or just telling you what you want to hear.  
The principle is simple but powerful: define your values clearly, then create situations that reveal whether candidates actually hold those values.

This chapter is about the single most important work you'll do before opening your doors. Not the fun stuff like choosing equipment or designing your space. The hard stuff: defining your mission, vision, and core values.

## **The Mistake Most Squatpreneurs Make**

Here's how most people approach culture:

* Open the practice  
* Hire some people  
* Get busy seeing patients  
* Realise the culture isn't quite right  
* Try to retrofit values and mission statements  
* Wonder why nobody takes them seriously

I've seen this play out dozens of times. A practice owner gets frustrated with their team's attitude or behaviour. So they decide to create "core values." They have a team meeting, put up a nice poster, maybe even print it on mugs.  
Nothing changes.  
Why? Because culture isn't created by posters. Culture is created by decisions. And those decisions start from day one.

If you compromise on a hire because you're desperate to fill a position, you've made a cultural decision. You've decided that urgency matters more than alignment.

Culture is what you do, not what you say. But before you can do it consistently, you need to know what it is.  
That's why I spent hours on this work before opening. Not because I'm a perfectionist. Because I knew I'd only get one chance to build the foundation right.

## **What Mission, Vision, and Values Actually Are**

Let me clear up the confusion around these terms, because most people use them interchangeably. They're not the same thing:  
Mission = Why you exist. What problem you're solving. Who you serve.  
Vision = Where you're going. What you're building towards. Your aspirational future.  
Core Values = How you behave. Your non-negotiable principles. What you stand for.  
Think of it this way:

* Mission is your **purpose**  
* Vision is your **destination**  
* Values are your **compass**

You need all three. Mission without vision is directionless effort. Vision without mission is empty ambition. And both are meaningless without values to guide your decisions.  
Let me show you what I drafted for Avenue Dental before we opened. I'm going to share these exactly as I wrote them at the time – not because they're perfect (they're not), but because they're a useful illustration of what "day one" versions of these statements typically look like. Later in this chapter, once I've walked you through how to build each element properly, I'll show you how these evolved over five years of actually running the practice.  
Here's where I started.  
**Day One Mission:** "It is our business mission to be the leading dental practice in the area for patient experience. To strive to remove barriers that can get in the way when it comes to a patients' ability to maintain a healthy smile. The Avenue Dental team will be constantly working to discover new ways to break through these barriers so that each one of our patients can achieve the smile they deserve."  
**Day One Vision:** "It is our mission to create a successful, financially rewarding private dental practice that will exceed expectations by providing exceptional dental care to our patients, unparalleled working environment to all our staff members and a business asset long term. We are passionate about what we do, and we want our patients to feel confident that they will receive the best care dentistry has to offer."

## **How to Create Your Mission: The "Why We Exist" Statement**

Your mission answers one fundamental question: Why does your practice exist?  
Not "to make money" (that's an outcome, not a purpose). Not "to provide dental care" (that's too generic). Your mission is the specific problem you solve for a specific group of people.  
Here's the framework I used:  
**Step 1: Identify Your Primary Audience.** Who are you serving? Be specific.

* Families in your local area?  
* Nervous patients who've avoided dentistry?  
* People wanting comprehensive care under one roof?  
* Professionals seeking convenient, high-quality care?

For Avenue Dental, I identified: Local families and individuals wanting exceptional patient experience and relationship-based care.  
**Step 2: Define Their Main Problem.** What's getting in their way? What frustrates them about current dental care?  
For my audience: Barriers to maintaining oral health – accessibility, trust, fear, inconsistent care, feeling like a number rather than a person.  
**Step 3: Articulate Your Solution.** How will you solve this differently?  
For Avenue Dental: By removing barriers, building long-term relationships, focusing relentlessly on patient experience, making dentistry accessible and trusted.  
**Step 4: Write It Down.** Combine these elements into a clear statement. Don't worry about making it poetic. Clarity matters more than cleverness.  
Your turn to draft yours:  
We exist to serve [AUDIENCE] by solving [PROBLEM] through [YOUR UNIQUE APPROACH].  
This becomes your mission. The "why" behind everything you do.

## **How to Create Your Vision: The "Where We're Going" Picture**

Your vision is aspirational. It's what you're building towards. Where you want to be in 5–10 years.  
But here's the key: Your vision should inspire your team, not just describe business goals.  
"To have 300 patients by year one" isn't a vision. That's an objective. Your vision should paint a picture of the practice you're creating and the impact you'll have.  
Here's the framework:  
**Step 1: Imagine Your Ideal Future.** Five years from now, what does success look like?

* What's the practice atmosphere?  
* How do patients feel?  
* What's the team culture like?  
* What's your reputation in the community?  
* How does working there feel?

**Step 2: Define Success Beyond Revenue.** Yes, financial success matters. But what else?

* Quality of care you deliver?  
* Team environment you've created?  
* Impact on your community?  
* The business asset you've built?

For Avenue Dental, I defined success as: Exceptional dental care + unparalleled working environment + community impact + valuable business asset.  
**Step 3: Make It Aspirational But Believable.** Your vision should stretch you without being fantasy.  
"To be the best practice in the world" is too vague and unrealistic.  
"To be the leading practice in our area for patient experience" is specific and achievable.  
**Step 4: Write Your Vision.** Paint the picture of what you're building. Make it something your team can see themselves being part of.  
Draft yours:  
We are building [TYPE OF PRACTICE] that will [IMPACT ON PATIENTS], create [TYPE OF ENVIRONMENT] for our team, and [LONG-TERM OUTCOME].

## **How to Create Your Core Values: The "How We Behave" Principles**

This is where most people go wrong. They choose aspirational words that sound good but mean nothing.  
"Excellence. Innovation. Integrity."  
Great. What does that actually mean in practice? How do these values guide decisions?  
Here's how to create values that actually matter:  
**Step 1: Identify Your Non-Negotiables.** What principles are you absolutely unwilling to compromise on? Even when it's inconvenient or expensive?  
For me, it was:

* We will never put profit before what's right for patients  
* We will treat every patient like family  
* We will be honest, even when it's uncomfortable  
* We will prioritise healthcare outcomes over cosmetic revenue

**Step 2: Make Them Behavioural.** Each value should translate into specific behaviours.  
Don't just say "Integrity." Say what integrity means in your practice.  
For Avenue Dental, I created "BE" statements that made values actionable:  
BE DEDICATED to treating patients, not just teeth. Take time to know each patient. Build long-term relationships. Focus on improving oral health, not just fixing problems.  
BE COMMITTED to excellence whilst providing highest quality care. Put prevention at the core.  
BE COMPASSIONATE because compassion and quality go hand in hand. Create a team focused on patient experience.  
BE RENOWNED by building a brand well integrated in the local community.  
**Step 3: Test Against Decisions.** Your values should help you make tough decisions. If a value doesn't guide decisions, it's just decoration.  
Test each value:

* Would this help you decide between two candidates?  
* Would this help you choose how to handle a difficult patient situation?  
* Would this guide a pricing decision?  
* Would this influence how you handle a team conflict?

If a value doesn't help with real decisions, remove it.  
**Step 4: Keep Them Few.** Three to five core values maximum. More than that and nobody remembers them.  
My day-one list had six. That's one more than I'd recommend to you now – and as you'll see shortly, I eventually tightened mine down too. But even with one too many, each of the six genuinely guided decisions in those early years, which is the only thing that really matters about a values list.  
Your turn:  
List 3–5 non-negotiable principles. For each one, write:

* The value itself  
* What it means behaviourally  
* A specific decision it would guide

## **How to Embed Your Mission, Vision, and Values (Not Just Frame Them)**

Creating these statements is step one. Making them actually matter is the hard part.  
Here's how to embed them into everything you do:

## **In Hiring Decisions**

Your opportunity marketing (Chapter 8) should reflect your mission and values. Your interview questions (Chapter 9) should test for cultural alignment.  
When I interview candidates now, I literally ask: "Here are our core values. Which one resonates most with you? Can you give me an example of when you've lived that value?"  
If they can't answer or seem confused, they're not the right cultural fit. No matter how qualified they are.

## **In Onboarding**

Your new hires should learn your mission, vision, and values on day one. Not as a boring lecture, but as the foundation of everything.  
At Avenue Dental, our induction document starts with mission and vision. Before we teach systems or procedures, we teach "why we exist" and "where we're going."  
New team members should be able to explain your mission in their own words by the end of week one.

## **In Daily Decisions**

This is where most practices fail. They create beautiful mission statements, then ignore them when making decisions.  
Your mission, vision, and values should guide:

* Treatment planning decisions  
* Pricing decisions  
* Patient communication  
* Team conflicts  
* Resource allocation  
* Schedule management

Every significant decision should pass the culture test: "Does this align with our mission, vision, and values?"  
If it doesn't, you either change the decision or acknowledge you're compromising your culture. At least be honest about it.

## **In Team Meetings**

Reference your mission and values regularly. Not in a preachy way, but as the framework for discussions.  
"Remember, our mission is to remove barriers to oral health. How does our current appointment system either support or undermine that?"  
"One of our core values is treating patients like family. Are we living that value in our recall system?"  
Make culture part of the conversation, not just the wall art.

## **In Performance Reviews**

Team members should be evaluated on cultural alignment, not just technical competence.  
Someone might be technically brilliant but culturally toxic. That's not A-player material.  
Someone might be still developing technically but embody your values perfectly. That's someone worth investing in.

## **The Hard Truth About Culture**

Here's what nobody tells you about building culture:  
It's easy to create mission, vision, and values. It's hard to live them consistently.  
You'll face moments where the right cultural decision is expensive or inconvenient. That's when culture becomes real.  
When a patient demands treatment you know isn't right for them and refusing means losing revenue – that's when "ethics before profits" either means something or it doesn't.  
When you need to hire someone desperately, and you have a qualified candidate who doesn't quite fit your values – that's when your values either guide you or get ignored.  
When your most productive team member creates a toxic environment – that's when your commitment to "unparalleled working environment" either matters or it's just marketing.  
Culture is built in these moments. Not in the easy decisions, but in the hard ones.

## **What Five Years of Running a Practice Taught Me**

I promised at the start of this chapter that I'd show you how my original mission, vision and values evolved over time. Here's the current version, after five years of actually running Avenue Dental:  
**Current Mission:** "To remove the barriers – fear, cost, time, past experience – that stop people from looking after their teeth. And to make the process feel like being cared for by family."  
**Current Vision:** "A practice our patients choose us for the relationship as much as the dentistry. A team who still want to work here in ten years' time. A standard of care the community measures every other practice by."  
**Current Values:**

* Patient care over profit – we do what's right for patients, even when it costs us  
* Team over individual – we support each other's success  
* Growth mindset – we see challenges as learning opportunities  
* Authentic communication – we say what we mean, with kindness

Notice what's different.  
The values went from six to four, and every one of them now starts with a behavioural principle rather than an abstract virtue. "Honesty" became "Authentic communication – we say what we mean, with kindness." That's a value I can test for in an interview, use to resolve a team conflict, and apply to a patient conversation. "Honesty" on its own is just a word on a wall.  
"Treating patients like family" and "Ethics before profits" collapsed into "Patient care over profit – we do what's right for patients, even when it costs us." Same spirit, tighter wording, and the "even when it costs us" phrase is what makes it a real commitment rather than a platitude.  
The original value I called "Healthcare – emphasising overall health care" quietly dropped off the list entirely, because when I looked at it honestly I realised it wasn't actually guiding any of my decisions. It was there because it sounded like something a dental practice should say.  
The mission and vision went through the same tightening. The original mission was trying to say "we remove barriers to healthy smiles" – which was good – but it said it in corporate language that wouldn't survive being read aloud at a team meeting. The refined version names the actual barriers (fear, cost, time, past experience) and commits to a specific feeling (being cared for by family). That's something a team member can repeat. That's something a patient can recognise.  
The vision stopped trying to do four jobs at once. Instead of listing financial success and team environment and business asset and patient care, it picks three measurable human outcomes: patient relationships, team longevity, and community reputation. If those three things are true in ten years, everything else follows.  
I'm showing you all of this for one reason: whatever you write before opening will not be your final version. And that's fine. It's not supposed to be. The draft you write today is the foundation that your real-world experience will refine. You're not supposed to get this perfect. You're supposed to start.  
So start.

# **The Trust Timeline**

I'll never forget our first patient phone call at Avenue Dental.  
We'd been open for maybe three hours. The phone rang. I looked at my receptionist. She looked at me. We both froze for a second like teenagers at a school dance.  
Then I lunged for it. "Avenue Dental, good morning!"  
It was someone wanting to book a checkup. A completely normal, routine call. But we celebrated like we'd won the lottery.  
Because we had no idea if anyone would actually ring. We'd built this beautiful practice. Hired a small team. Opened the doors. And then ... we waited.  
I opened with about a hundred people interested in becoming patients. That sounds decent, right? It kept us afloat. But here's what I've learned since then:  
It wasn't enough. And more importantly, I'd done it the hard way.  
Since opening Avenue Dental, I've been teaching other dentists how to open squat practices through Squat Success. And as I've refined the methodology, I've watched my students open with 300–400 people already queuing up. Some of the social-media-savvy ones? They're opening with 700–800 people hammering at their doors, ready to spend big money.  
The difference? They're following a systematic approach to building what I call the Invisible Waiting List. Something I stumbled into accidentally but have now perfected into a repeatable system.  
This chapter is about the single biggest mistake most squatpreneurs make: waiting until after they open to start attracting patients.

## **The Traditional Marketing Trap**

Here's the typical squat practice timeline:  
Month 1–6: Find location, negotiate lease, plan the build  
Month 7–12: Construction, equipment installation, CQC prep, hiring  
Month 13: Opening day!  
Month 13.5: Panic. Where are all the patients?  
Month 14: Emergency marketing budget. Expensive ads. Expensive agency. Expensive disappointment.  
Sound familiar? This is what most practice owners do. Build in secret, open with fanfare, then desperately try to convince strangers to trust them.  
The problem? Trust doesn't work on demand.  
You can't spend thousands on slick ads trying to manufacture trust with people who've never heard of you. That's not how human psychology works.  
But here's what does work: Building trust whilst you're building your practice.

## **The Trust Timeline Reversal**

**Traditional approach:** Spend money → Open practice → Try to build trust → Hope patients come  
**The Invisible Waiting List approach:** Build trust → Build following → Open practice → Welcome eager patients  
This single shift changes everything.  
When I opened Avenue Dental, my hundred interested people came mostly from location visibility. We were on a prominent site. I put up banners, QR codes, made the building impossible to miss. People saw us being built and enquired.  
That worked. But it was passive. It relied entirely on foot traffic and visibility.  
Since then, I've developed a more proactive system. And the dentists following this system properly are opening with 3–4x the interested patients I had, without spending anything on traditional advertising.  
Let me show you how it works.

## **The Three Pillars of Pre-Opening Trust**

### **Pillar 1: Location Visibility (What I Did)**

If you're lucky enough to have a prominent location like I did, maximise it.  
Don't be timid. Be bold. Be flamboyant. This is your one chance to make noise without worrying about being "too much."  
One of my Squat Success members in Norwich did this brilliantly. She branded her floor-to-ceiling glass windows in a way that's absolutely impossible to miss. Striking. Bold. Unapologetic.  
The feedback and enquiries she's getting are incredible. Because she wasn't afraid to stand out.  
Here's what worked for location-based visibility:  
Large, clear signage: Not subtle. LARGE. Make it visible from the road.  
QR codes everywhere: On windows, on construction barriers, on signage. Make it dead easy for curious people to find out more.  
Progress updates: People are naturally curious about construction. Give them something to follow – even if it's just a sign saying, "Opening February 2026 – Scan for updates."  
Window displays: Before you open, use your windows to tell your story. Who you are, what you stand for, why you're different.  
This passive approach works if you have the location. But most people don't have prime high-street visibility. Which brings us to the more powerful approaches ...

### **Pillar 2: Community Integration (What Works Better)**

The most effective tactic I've seen for building the Invisible Waiting List? Local community engagement. Specifically: Facebook community groups and local business integration.

### **Facebook Local Groups:**

Every area has local community Facebook groups. "Leamington Residents," "Norwich Families," "Mums of Manchester" – you know the ones.  
Most dentists are terrified to post in these groups. Why? Because they're scared of negative comments.  
"You're not NHS? You're just money-grabbers!" "Another private dentist we can't afford!" "Why aren't you helping the community by taking NHS patients?"  
I've seen my Squat Success members get absolutely bashed in these groups. It's brutal. Personal. Often unfair.  
Here's what I've learned: The ones who push through the negativity get WAY more enquiries and engagement than those who give up.  
Let me be direct about this: If you can't handle negative comments on a Facebook post, you're not going to survive business ownership. Seriously.  
Business involves criticism. Patient complaints. Staff disagreements. Supplier problems. Online reviews. If anonymous Facebook comments break you, the reality of practice ownership will destroy you.  
So here's what successful squatpreneurs do in local groups:

* Share their journey (building updates, why they're opening, their vision)  
* Offer value (quick dental tips, myth-busting, answering questions)  
* Be transparent (costs, approach, who they're serving, why private)  
* Engage genuinely (respond to questions, ignore trolls, focus on curious people)

Yes, you'll get negativity. Ignore it. The people who matter – your future patients – are watching how you handle it. Grace under fire builds trust.

### **Local Business Integration:**

This is subtler but powerful. Connect with local businesses before you open.  
Coffee shops, gyms, kids' activity centres, estate agents, local retailers – these businesses already have the community relationships you're trying to build.  
Some approaches that work:

* Offer to provide dental health content for their newsletters  
* Partner on community events  
* Cross-promote (you mention them, they mention you)  
* Create referral relationships  
* Sponsor local sports teams or school events

You're not asking them to sell for you. You're integrating into the existing community fabric.

### **Pillar 3: Personal Content Creation (What Works Best)**

This is where the real magic happens. And where most dentists completely fail because they're terrified of putting themselves out there.  
Here's the truth: People don't trust practices. They trust people.  
When someone's choosing a dentist, they're not really choosing a building or a brand. They're choosing a person they'll trust with their health, their appearance, their vulnerabilities.  
Professional marketing makes you look competent. Personal content makes people trust you.  
Let me show you the difference:

### **Professional Marketing Approach:**

* Hire designer for logo  
* Get professional photos taken  
* Create slick website  
* Run polished ads  
* Cost: £5,000–£10,000+  
* Result: You look professional but impersonal  
* Problem: Stops working when you stop paying

### **Personal Content Approach:**

* Share your journey building the practice  
* Document decisions you're making  
* Explain the "why" behind your choices  
* Show your personality and values  
* Cost: £0 (just your time)  
* Result: People feel like they know you  
* Advantage: Content works forever and compounds

I see this pattern consistently: The dentists who embrace personal content creation open with 2–3x more patients than those who rely on traditional marketing.

### **The Edutainment Formula**

"But Bobby, what do I post? I'm not an influencer!"  
You don't need to be. You just need to share useful, interesting content about what you're doing.  
I call this "edutainment" – education plus entertainment. It works in dentistry because education removes fear barriers, entertainment makes it shareable, and authenticity builds the trust that converts viewers into patients.  
The specific mechanics of what to post, how often, and in what format – we'll cover all of that in the next chapter. For now, the point is simply this: you can't start too early, and you don't need to be an expert at it to begin.  
This third pillar – personal content creation – is substantial enough that the next two chapters are dedicated to it. Chapter 12 will show you exactly what to post, how often, and what system makes it sustainable. Chapter 13 will cover how to actually get in front of the camera, and how to convert the followers you build into booked appointments.  
For the purposes of this chapter, the point is simply this: you need to start creating content now. Not because you feel ready. Not because you've figured out the perfect system. Now. Because every week you delay is a week the trust you could have been building hasn't been built.

## **Tracking Your Progress**

How do you know if this is working? Here are the metrics that matter:  
Engagement Rate: Are people commenting, sharing, saving your posts? Engagement matters more than follower count. 100 engaged followers beat 1,000 passive ones.  
Direct Messages: Are people DMing you with questions? This is gold – direct conversation builds the strongest relationships.

Local Recognition: Are people stopping you to say, "I've been following your practice build"? This face-to-face validation means your content is working.  
Community Response: In Facebook groups, are more people engaging positively than negatively? Are others defending you against trolls? That's your community forming.  
Don't obsess over vanity metrics like total followers. Focus on genuine interest. I'd rather have 200 people ready to book than 2,000 passive scrollers.

## **The Compounding Effect**

Here's what makes this approach so powerful: It compounds over time.  
Traditional ads: You pay £1,000 this month, you get visibility this month. Stop paying, stop getting results.  
Authentic content: You create content this month, it works forever. Someone discovers you in 6 months, they can binge your entire journey, get to know you, and book immediately.

That's the compound effect in action.

# **Your Personal Brand Blueprint**

## **The "What Do I Post?" Problem**

So here we are. You know why you need to be building trust before opening. You know the three pillars. You've accepted that personal content creation is the most powerful of the three. Now the question that's been sitting there since the last chapter: *What do I actually post?*  
This is the number one objection I hear from dentists. "I'm not interesting." "My day is boring." "Nobody wants to hear about dental stuff." "I'm not an influencer."  
Let me stop you right there.  
You're not building a personal brand to become an influencer. You're not trying to get famous or go viral. You're simply letting potential patients see the real dentist behind the practice.  
That's it. That's the whole game.  
And here's what I've learned: you don't need to be interesting. You just need to be interested. Interested in your work, interested in your patients, interested in building something meaningful.  
When you share that interest authentically, people connect with it.  
This chapter is your blueprint for creating content consistently without it taking over your life. Because you're building a dental practice, not a social media empire.  
Most dentists freeze because they're overthinking it.  
They think every post needs to be brilliant, perfectly polished, strategically planned. So they post nothing. Or they post once, get modest results, and give up.  
Here's what actually works: Document, don't create.  
This concept changed everything for me. I learned it from Gary Vaynerchuk, and it's the single most liberating approach to content.  
You're not trying to CREATE content from scratch every day. You're simply DOCUMENTING what you're already doing.  
Building a practice? That's content. Choosing equipment? That's content. Interviewing staff? That's content. Making design decisions? That's content. Having doubts? That's content. Celebrating wins? That's content.  
Your life as a squatpreneur IS the content. You just need to capture and share it.

## **The Five Content Pillars**

But how do you know what to document? What actually matters to potential patients?  
I use five content pillars. Every piece of content falls into one of these categories. This framework ensures you're not just posting randomly but building a complete picture of who you are.

### **Pillar 1: Clinical Excellence**

This is the "I know what I'm doing" pillar.  
You're demonstrating your clinical knowledge and staying current with dentistry. But you're doing it in a way that's accessible, not intimidating.

#### **Examples:**

* "Here's why I chose this particular composite system for the practice ..."  
* "Just completed a course on minimally invasive dentistry. Here's what I learned ..."  
* "This is how I explain root canals to nervous patients ..."  
* "Quick tip: This is the proper way to floss (most people get it wrong) ..."

The key: Don't use jargon. Explain things simply. Show your expertise through clarity, not complexity.

#### **Practical approach:**

* Take a 30-second video after courses explaining one takeaway  
* Share equipment decisions with the "why" behind them  
* Answer common patient questions you hear repeatedly  
* Show before/after work (with consent) and explain the process simply

### **Pillar 2: Patient Care**

This is the "I actually care about people" pillar. You're showing that you see patients as humans, not just teeth. This is what differentiates you from corporate dentistry.

#### **Examples:**

* "Here's how we've designed the waiting room to reduce anxiety ..."  
* "Why we spend 45 minutes on consultations instead of rushing through ..."  
* "The small touches that matter: how we remember patient preferences ..."  
* "Our approach to nervous patients – it's all about trust and time ..."

The key: Share your philosophy, not just your techniques. What do you believe about patient care? What matters to you?

#### **Practical approach:**

* Document your design decisions (colours, layout, atmosphere)  
* Share your consultation approach  
* Introduce team members and their patient care philosophy  
* Show the "invisible" things you do for patients

### **Pillar 3: Personal Journey**

This is the "I'm a real person" pillar. People connect with people, not practices. This pillar lets them get to know YOU.

#### **Examples:**

* "Why I decided to open a squat practice after years as an associate ..."  
* "The moment I nearly gave up on this project ..."  
* "What I'm learning about business that dental school never taught ..."  
* "My journey from hating video to doing it daily ..."

The key: Vulnerability builds connection. Share struggles, not just successes. People trust authenticity more than perfection.

#### **Practical approach:**

* Share your "why" story  
* Document challenges and how you're overcoming them  
* Talk about what you're learning  
* Show your personality and interests outside dentistry

### **Pillar 4: Community Connection**

This is the "I'm part of this community" pillar. You're not parachuting in to extract money. You're integrating into the local fabric.

#### **Examples:**

* "Visited this amazing local coffee shop during our build – supporting local matters ..."  
* "Our practice sources from these local suppliers ..."  
* "Sponsoring the under-12 football team this season ..."  
* "Here's what I love about this neighbourhood ..."

The key: Genuine integration, not performative community involvement. Support local because you believe in it, not for marketing points.

#### **Practical approach:**

* Feature local businesses you work with  
* Participate in community events (and share it)  
* Highlight local team members  
* Show how you're contributing to the area

### **Pillar 5: Educational Value**

This is the "I'm helping even before they're patients" pillar. You're providing value with zero expectation of return. This builds massive goodwill.

#### **Examples:**

* "Three signs you might need to see a dentist urgently ..."  
* "Myth: You should brush right after eating. Reality: Wait 30 minutes. Here's why ..."  
* "What that weird taste in your mouth might actually mean ..."  
* "How to choose an electric toothbrush (simple guide) ..."

The key: Make it genuinely useful. Give away good information freely. Trust that value creates reciprocity.

#### **Practical approach:**

* Answer the questions patients always ask  
* Bust common dental myths  
* Share prevention tips
